In two days, Americans voting in the midterm elections will decide control of the U.S. House and Senate for the next two years.

Republicans are defending their slim congressional majorities, but President Donald Trump predicts the GOP will hold the House and Senate.

"If you elect a Republican House and a Republican Senate on November 3rd, we will continue to put America first every single day," the president pledged on the campaign trail last week. And he claimed if the Democrats take control, "this country will go to hell."

Republicans are fighting political history, as the party in power in Washington has traditionally lost seats during midterm election years. The GOP also faces a challenging political climate fueled by persistent inflation tied in part to what polls show is an unpopular war with Iran and Trump's increasingly negative approval ratings.

Also up for grabs in this year's midterm elections — governorships in 36 of the 50 states (as well as the territories of Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands and the U.S. Virgin Islands). Plus, all but four states are holding legislative elections. And voters will also weigh in on 145 statewide ballot measures in states from coast to coast.

Here's a closer look at what's up for grabs on Election Day 2026.

SENATE

Republicans currently control the chamber 53-47, with the final Fox News Power Rankings ahead of Election Day rating the race for Senate control a toss-up.

Both parties are three seats short of a majority, according to the forecast. Democrats are favored in 48 seats and Republicans are ahead in 47. The GOP needs to hold 50 seats to retain control of the chamber, since Republican Vice President JD Vance is the tie-breaking vote in the Senate.

If Democrats ride a blue wave to victory by winning most or all of the competitive races, they could potentially come away from the midterms with up to 54 seats. But if Republicans have a good night and defy expectations, the GOP could hold the majority with up to 52 seats, according to the forecast.

Republicans are playing defense in Alaska, Iowa, Maine, South Carolina and Texas, which are all rated toss-ups in the Power Rankings. They're also defending seats in competitive races in Kansas, which is rated Lean Republican, and Ohio, which is rated Lean Democrat.

Democrats are on defense in Michigan, which is rated a toss-up, and in Minnesota and New Hampshire, which are listed as Lean Democrat.

HOUSE OF REPRESENTATIVES

The GOP is hanging on to a razor-thin majority in the House, and the Power Rankings point to the Democrats retaking control of the chamber.

A good night for the Democrats could see them ending up with as many as 240 seats in the 435-seat chamber. But if Republicans have a good night, they could end up with 212 seats. That’s six short of a majority, but enough to make life difficult for the next Democratic House Speaker, who would have to balance the demands of the moderate and far-left wings of the party.

BALLOT MEASURES

Referendums requiring voters to present identification when they show up the polls, or bans on noncitizen voting, are on the ballot in 11 states: Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, California, Colorado, Kansas, Nevada, North Carolina, Ohio, South Dakota and West Virginia.

Voters in Alaska will also decide if they want to repeal the state’s ranked choice voting system, open primaries and a campaign finance disclosure rule. Voters approve the current system in 2020 and narrowly kept it in 2024.

But the voting system has come under fresh scrutiny amid the controversy over a potential spoiler candidate in Alaska's U.S. Senate race.

Voters in Virginia will decide whether the right to vote should be restored to convicted felons upon their release.

California's Proposition 40 is making national headlines.

The measure, the first of its kind, would slap a one-time 5% tax on wealth for Californians worth over $1 billion, with the vast majority of the revenue generated going to pay for healthcare.

Also in the spotlight is millionaires tax in Washington State. If passed, the referendum would repeal a 9.9% tax on income over $1 million a year, which state lawmakers recently approved. Washington State currently does not have a personal income tax.

Voters in Maryland, Missouri, and Colorado weigh in on ballot measures on congressional redistricting, as the mid-decade redistricting fight between Democrats and Republicans will be waged again in 2028.

Arizona, Colorado, Nebraska, and Washington State have referendums on transgender students’ participation in sports. Colorado also has a referendum on blocking gender transition surgeries for minors and preventing state or federal funding from being used for those operations.

Voters in Missouri weigh in on repealing a right to abortion and banning gender transition medical care for children. Meanwhile, and Idaho initiative would overturn one of the nation's strictest abortion bans, while Virginia and Nevada voters decide on establishing a right to abortion in their state constitutions.

California, Colorado, and Arizona hold ballot measures on policy around energy, transportation and development.

Why everybody still loves 'Raymond' 30 years later: How suburban family chaos became comedy gold

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When "Everybody Loves Raymond" debuted 30 years ago, it showed how a stand-up comic from Queens and a series about suburban in-laws could thrive on primetime network television.

Three decades, nine seasons and 69 Emmy nominations later, the classic sitcom has stood the test of time and remains a comfort show for generations of viewers.

Now, as star Ray Romano and his longtime manager, Rory Rosegarten — an executive producer on the show — prepare for induction into the Long Island Music and Entertainment Hall of Fame, the show's stars and creative team are reflecting on the real-life experiences that helped make it a lasting success.

‘FAMILY AFFAIR’ STAR KATHY GARVER SAYS FAILED REMAKE ‘SUCKED,’ REVEALS WHAT WENT WRONG

Speaking with Fox News Digital, Patricia Heaton said "Everybody Loves Raymond" reflected her own experiences with marriage and motherhood, making it more than just another Hollywood sitcom pilot.

"It wasn’t an overnight success; it was a humble family comedy that, over the years, has resonated worldwide," Heaton said. "It wasn’t flashy, just incredibly funny and relatable. I found it mirrored my own personal life with my marriage and kids, which is how you know it was going to be relatable to a huge audience."

Heaton said the cast, including Romano, Brad Garrett, Doris Roberts, Peter Boyle, was "perfectly suited to their characters" and that each year the scripts got "funnier and funnier." She recalled table reads as some of her favorite days of the week.

"All those episodes continue to reflect the joys and struggles of families all around the world, from generation to generation," Heaton said. "It was a joy to perform, a joy to watch, and a joy to know that we brought a little bit of happiness to people’s lives."

But before the Barone family became a television favorite, the series hinged on a stand-up comic and the manager who discovered him at a New York radio station's comedy competition.

Rosegarten, Romano's longtime manager, first encountered the comedian while judging the competition. He was immediately drawn to Romano's grounded, self-deprecating routine about his wife and kids.

"When I saw Ray that first time, it was love at first sight," Rosegarten told Fox News Digital. "He was so original, and he was talking about real life, not made-up stuff. Not a piece of hair on a soap, but he was talking about his wife and his kids and his life. I wish I could tell you I knew what would happen to him, would happen. I don’t think anybody could predict that. But I thought he was really funny."

Rosegarten said he later used his connections to secure Romano an appearance on the "Late Show with David Letterman."

LORNE MICHAELS’ ‘SNL’ GRIP ON CAST EXPOSED IN NEW DOCUMENTARY

"He went on the show, and he killed," Rosegarten recalled. "He killed in such a way that to this day I still use that footage to show people exactly how to do it. Like, that should be the guideline."

That appearance helped pave the way for a CBS pilot, though Rosegarten said getting the family sitcom on network television also came down to luck.

"We shot a pilot for CBS, and Les Moonves, who then was the president of CBS, literally took the tape home over the weekend," Rosegarten said. "It’s pilot season, and I’m sure he had a million shows to watch. But he watched that one with his family and they liked it."

"I lay in bed sometimes, and I think if he had been late that night for a dinner, or he had been fighting with someone in the office, and he didn't take the tape, I might not be talking to you right now," he added. "So there's a little luck involved, too."

'HOME IMPROVEMENT' STAR SAYS PATRICIA RICHARDSON GOT 'TIRED' OF FIGHTING FOR SITCOM

"Everybody Loves Raymond" premiered Sept. 13, 1996, and ran for nine seasons before its finale in 2005. The sitcom won 15 Emmy Awards and followed Italian American sportswriter Ray Barone as he navigated marriage, fatherhood and the interference of his parents and brother, who lived across the street on Long Island.

Rosegarten credited much of the show's success to its writing, which drew heavily from the real-life experiences of the writing staff and cast. He recalled a rule established by creator and showrunner Phil Rosenthal that discouraged late nights in the writers' room.

"We were one of the very few shows where everybody went home at six o’clock," Rosegarten said. "Because Phil’s attitude was: 'Go home, have a fight with your wife, we’ll talk about it tomorrow, and we’ll write about it.' And that was a stroke of genius, actually."

That emphasis on everyday family life helped revitalize the traditional sitcom, according to Ron Simon, head curator at the Paley Center for Media.

"The importance of 'Everybody Loves Raymond' is that it revitalized the family situation comedy," Simon told Fox News Digital. "It debuted in 1996 and it sort of brought a whole new life to one of the staples of television comedy from the very beginning. 'Everybody Loves Raymond' was able to take a look at all these different frustrations of suburban life in Long Island and really create a cast that anyone watching could identify with."

Simon pointed to the show's familiar family dynamics as a key part of its appeal.

"They took frustrations that happened in their own lives and then adapted them for the situation comedy, so it was very real," Simon said. "No matter who you were, whether it was domestic squabbles between Ray and Debra, the intrusiveness of the mother and father. And of course, there’s always sibling rivalry between Ray and Robert. So, it just had a great formula."

The show's Long Island roots are now being recognized as Romano and Rosegarten prepare to enter the Long Island Music and Entertainment Hall of Fame. The pair is being honored as the institution's first non-music inductees.

The Curious Case of Benjamin Button review: The real mystery of this musical is why

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Perhaps when you think of The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, you think of Brad Pitt, who headlined David Fincher's 2008 film adaptation to the tune of 13 Oscar nominations (though no major category wins). That movie took major liberties with the 1922 F. Scott Fitzgerald short story on which it's based. So does the Olivier Award-winning new musical, which has just opened Off-Broadway at the Public Theater.

And that's for the best, as Fitzgerald's short story was mean and odd. Alas, the movie and musical made from it are less of both, and yet, I yearn for more.

What remains the same across all three iterations is this: Benjamin Button is born old and ages in reverse. Thus, his death is basically going from zygote to nothingness.

SEE ALSO: 'Copperfield! The New Musical' review: A rollicking, big-hearted Dickens adaptation that is not to be missed

In the case of the musical, writer/lyricist Jethro Compton and composer/lyricist Darren Clark transport the story from 1860s Baltimore to a Cornish fishing village at the end of World War I. There, a quirky village consists of a baker with a wandering eye, a drunken tea shop proprietor, a woman who dreams of growing her lawn gnome into a husband (stranger things have happened), and Roger Button (Brandon J. Ellis), an expectant father who is not expecting to find his newborn son is an old man with a cap and cane.

Andrew Durand stars as Benjamin Button.

Andrew Durand and Kelsee Kimmel (foreground) with the company of "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button." Credit: Joan Marcus

Earlier this year, Durand played Brad Majors in Broadway's Rocky Horror Show revival. In The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, he thrusts his jaw forward and stoops his shoulders to convey that the eponymous hero is effectively a septuagenarian at birth. Of course, as Benjamin ages in reverse, Durand will be tasked with shifting his physicality to that of a man in his fifties, who is besotted by a twentysomething barmaid, Elowen Keene (Kelsee Kimmel). As she grows older, he'll grow more spry and lively, dancing and frolicking with children, then running away like one.

SEE ALSO: 'Memoirs of Amorous Gentlemen' review: Duncan Sheik's musical adaptation gets kink right, but falters elsewhere

His performance is broad and cartoonish, framing the musical as a farce. Around him, the ensemble, who play multiple supporting characters along with instruments throughout, match this tone with relish. The music by Clark is rollicking, like a country fair. "Rag Ty Yw Tre" opens the show, setting up a theme of time and tide being fickle friends to man. Other songs will invoke nature too, like "The Western Wind," "The Kraken's Lullaby," and "The Tide Is Comin' In."

While the musical has garnered praise since its Off-West End debut to its West End run to its current Off-Broadway run at the Public, I struggled to make sense of the fervor for it. The orchestration was loud and muddled; I struggled to make out the lyrics outside of the chorus. The voices of the company are melodic, and their playing enthusiastic, yet neither can distract that many of Clark and Compton's songs are not just reprisals, but repetitive. "Time is a funny thing" is a sentiment repeated over and over, making the two-and-a-half-hour production feel longer and tiresome.

Benjamin Button is a bastard.

The company of "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button." Credit: Joan Marcus

This is true to the Fitzgerald story too. In both, Benjamin marries a younger woman, and then flees her as she grows old and he grows young. Compton and Clark's musical offers a different motivation than Fitzgerald's, but their revision makes his trespass even crueler. And disturbingly, they doesn't seem to realize how much of a cad their hero is. Or else they give him a maudlin backstory as justification.

In the short story, Benjamin's father urges him to play with other children. In this musical, his mother and father lock him in the attic, ashamed of his bizarre existence. After years of such neglect, Benjamin takes to sneaking out on Friday nights to a pub to experience community and beer. There, he meets his future bride, though much drama will come before they wed. Then, tragedy will strike their family, and he'll flee.

It's not that The Curious Case of Benjamin Button doesn't recognize that running away from your wife in a time of shared heartbreak isn't horrid. But rather that the musical's rush to forgive him is not just alarming but repulsive. Opposite Durand, Kimmel offers a performance that is charming and tender, while punctuated with occasional comedic bravado to keep in the musical's overall tone. But Elowen is a character thin as tissue paper, who exists purely to satisfy Benjamin's every need, be it love, forgiveness, reflection, or beer. Yet I felt for her as her love runs off on a vague journey of self-improvement that is a circle.

This dreadful second act lumbers into a confounding climax. Songs haphazardly tie up the loose ends of every clumsy thread of character arc, from horny baker to wannabe gnome wife. Flimsy characters flit around as Benjamin is treated as some profound figure, while his behavior is generally that of a fuckboy. By the end, I had the lyric "It's all just a matter of time" ringing in my head, as I felt I'd wasted mine.

The Curious Case of Benjamin Button is now playing at the Public Theater until Dec. 20.

How Pures 24-hour chat timer forces daters to skip the endless banter

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

One of the major annoyances of online dating is how often matches devolve into pen pals rather than translate into IRL dates. Those initial, flirty conversations are great, don't get us wrong, but most of us aren't jumping onto Tinder or Hinge just to endlessly text. And yet, in modern dating, where both people often feel afraid to make the first move and take the match into the real world, endless texting is all too often the only result of a once-promising match. 

But it doesn't have to be this way. Dating app Pure concocted some clever solutions to the never-ending chatting, and in our own experimentation, they work like a charm. 

Here's how Pure introduced a sense of urgency into its dating mechanics, and how that helps users translate matches into real-life encounters. 

Opens in a new window Credit: Pure Pure   Learn More

How Pure leverages a sense of urgency to benefit its users

One unique feature of Pure is that it doesn't use traditional dating profiles, which means its matches operate differently than on basically every other dating app. 

Instead of having a permanent, public-facing profile, Pure users can post "ads," temporary personal advertisements shouting out who they are or what they want, whether that's for a long-term relationship or a short-term connection. It's only when someone has posted an ad that they're discoverable to other users, and only then are your basic profile elements (age, gender, sexual orientation, and photos) visible. Once your ad expires, so does access to your profile.

The result of this mechanic is a sense of urgency, the very real possibility that you're seeing someone you may never see again, so if you want a connection, you have to act fast. That's a very welcome change from the dating app norm, where profiles are always visible, and you can browse them at your convenience, whether on your morning commute or between sets at the gym.

But Pure takes things a step further with how you actually interact with other people. 

The disappearing chat mechanic

From the moment you initiate a chat on Pure, you're under an artificial time constraint, since every chat automatically self-deletes after 24 hours, unless both parties agree to suspend the chat timer. 

While at first this seems like an unnecessary constraint, in practice it's actually very healthy and helps daters avoid the endless banter that plagues so many online dating interactions. 

Think about what would happen if you approached a cute stranger in the real world. You may see them at a bar or coffee shop, going about their day, but no matter how smooth your introduction, you know in the back of your mind that you can't monopolize this person's time and attention for very long. 

For an in-person approach, you have to be concise in your communication: I think you're cute and want a chance to get to know you better — would that interest you? But on an app, where there's no sense of urgency, you can endlessly waffle, delaying the most important question of all in favor of a never-ending series of mundane questions. 

This is why Pure's disappearing chat mechanic is so clever, even when both parties agree to disable it, since even that act forces a mutual acknowledgment of the connection. And after using the app over an extended period, we can confirm that Pure's culture really encourages in-person interactions at the expense of endless chatting or swiping.

NFL keeps fining Caleb Williams for refusing to cover logo of "science" cleats worn by over 80 players

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The NFL says player safety is a top priority, but apparently, wearing cleats designed to help prevent injuries can cost you a pretty penny.

Chicago Bears quarterback Caleb Williams has been fined a whopping $35,823 over two games for wearing Caddix cleats, a company only a few years old with only four employees, which claims its technology can reduce the risk of non-contact injuries through its SmartStuds technology.

CEO Jack Rasmussen, after explaining that Williams is not, and has not been a Caddix sponsored athlete, nor has he ever been paid by the company, told OutKick, "I get why he was fined, 100%. I totally get it. We're not a league sponsor, and we can't afford to be or want to be a league sponsor."

Williams was initially fined $11,941 for displaying an "unauthorized logo" during Chicago's Week 6 victory over the Atlanta Falcons. The NFL then doubled down, issuing another $23,882 fine for the same violation against the New England Patriots in Week 7.

While he understands why the league fined Williams, Rasmussen made his company's support extremely clear saying, "Caleb Williams bought [our cleats], liked them, wore them, and now he's being penalized for liking them and wearing them, and we're taking a stance where we're going to support a player who's looking out for his best interest."

Rasmussen also said, "We're a small company. We've been selling cleats for less than 2 years. I have a total of four employees, so this is certainly an uphill battle for us, but it's a battle we're happy to be a part of."

The interesting aspect of this whole saga is the fact that Caleb Williams has worn these cleats before the Bears Week 6 match-up against the Atlanta Falcons, but he wasn't fined. I reached out to the NFL for an explanation but have yet to hear a response.

NIKE CEO MAY HAVE JUST ADMITTED THE COMPANY WENT TOO WOKE AS STOCK HITS 12-YEAR LOW

In case you weren't aware, the reason you only see Adidas, Nike, and Under Armour logos on NFL players' feet is because the NFL can charge those brands tens of millions of dollars per year to be "official apparel partners" of the league. While players can wear cleats from other manufacturers, such as Caddix or New Balance, which is worn by Josh Allen, they are expected to wear non-branded cleats like Allen does.

Notably, in the same vein, San Francisco 49ers running back Christian McCaffrey wears Bruce Bolt gloves but the lightning bolt logo is absent from the glove, as well as the company name.

Rasmussen also told me that McCaffrey's teammate Kyle Juszczyk is one of 80 players that has, or continues to wear Caddix cleats. "We have over 80 athletes wearing them. We haven't paid them a dollar," Rasmussen stated. "We actually have players actively leaving and turning down endorsement deals from other companies to wear Caddix for free. It's not about us finding the biggest guy, it's about allowing guys to wear this cleat because it's better for them."

Other NFL players that wear Caddix cleats include: Cooper Kupp, Bubba Baker, Marlon Humphrey, Jalen Ramsey, Derek Stingley Jr., and Dalton Kincaid.

As I told Rasmussen, "Those are some pretty notable names." Kupp is a former Super Bowl MVP, coming off another Super Bowl win. Ramsey was the highest paid cornerback in NFL history a few short seasons ago. It's an impressive list, especially when brands are knocking at these athletes doors to represent their brand and products.

CARDINALS BACKUP CORNERBACK FINED BY NFL FOR WRITING 'GLORY BE 2 GOD' ON HIS EYEBLACK

As far as Rasmussen is aware, not a single one of these 80+ players have been fined by the NFL like Caleb Williams has. If they were, I'm sure he'd be inclined to pay their fine just as he and his company have offered for Williams'.

Speaking directly to the lack of fines for other players, Rasmussen said, "People have been wearing Caddix cleats in NFL games for 3 years. Joe Flacco wore them all last year, had that record breaking game actually against the Bears, ironically enough, and then he was on Netflix wearing Caddix logos all over television, and no one said a word. I think it's just very precarious timing, because you have this player in Caleb Williams, who we all know is gonna be the face of the league here pretty soon."

I also reached out to the NFL for an explanation as to why Caleb Williams has been the only NFL player fined for wearing Caddix cleats. I have yet to receive a response.

Cincinnati Bengals quarterback Joe Flacco, who began wearing Caddix cleats in 2024 and subsequently became an investor in the company, has offered to cover Williams' future fines.

"If Caleb gets fined again for wearing Caddix, I'll pay the fine myself," Flacco told ESPN. "This isn't about making a statement or breaking the rules just to break them. It's about wearing gear that works for you and helps keep you safe on the field."

But more good news for Caddix from a marketing standpoint, Caleb Williams is not backing down and has essentially become a non-paid spokesperson for the brand.

"I'm huge into trying to stay on the field. I'm huge into trying to be healthy," Williams told reporters responding to news of his fines. "They're smart cleats. They're science cleats, and they've been working for me."

So what are these "science cleats" Williams and other NFL players swear by?

Caddix uses patented SmartStuds technology, featuring flexible studs designed to reduce rotational stress on players' knees and ankles during sudden cuts and movements. Williams believes the technology can help prolong his career, particularly after recently returning from a hamstring injury.

Rasmussen also mentioned, "Several very high profile guys who are currently signed to shoe companies buy cleats on our website."

With that information, of course I had to ask if any of those athletes are Nike athletes, which Rasmussen responded, "Yes, I certainly cannot tell you who it is though."

Williams has appealed his initial fine and reportedly intends to continue wearing the cleats without covering the logo, which will continue to be a headache for the NFL giving Caddix free marketing, but due to NFL rules, that could lead to the league barring Williams from playing.

In case you weren't aware, the NFL has uniform inspectors at every game to make sure players like Williams comply with specific branding standards, or at least, are made aware when they don't comply, making the fines not a surprise if they refuse to comply.

According to the NFL, "The uniform inspector’s job is to make sure that every player in violation of the rules is aware of it and has an opportunity to correct it. He’ll talk directly to players and, a half-hour before kickoff, he’ll meet with a team representative to report problems. That employee must bring any player spotted with a violation back to the inspector for clearance before kickoff." The Uniform Inspections section of the NFL website further states, "Players and teams have the opportunity to get into compliance at the next change of possession or other "appropriate stoppage" in play. After the game, the inspector roams the locker rooms, checking to make sure that no non-partner logos can be seen by reporters, fans or television cameras — and then writes a summary of each game for the league. For many violations, a player cannot go back into the game until the problem is corrected. Teams can be penalized 5 yards if — after receiving a warning — the player enters the game anyway. Failing to comply with league warnings can result in hefty fines."

Specifically, the NFL's collective bargaining agreement, Article 51, Section 2(a), specifically gives the league authority to regulate third-party branding on players' footwear. They are a business, big business, and they want to protect their business interests. That makes sense.

But does the NFL actually have the ability to bar Williams from entering the game if he refuses to comply on Monday Night Football against the defending Super Bowl champion Seahawks?

According to the NFL's Official Playing Rules, Rule 5, Section 4, players must comply with league uniform and equipment standards, including restrictions on unauthorized branding. The rule's penalty provisions allow officials to prevent players from entering a game if they refuse to correct certain uniform violations before kickoff. Players who fail to correct violations discovered during a game can also be prohibited from returning to the field.

That authority is reinforced by federal court documentation. In National Football League Management Council v. NFL Players Association (2016), the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit reproduced the NFL's uniform enforcement policy, which explicitly stated: "All uniform and On Field violations detected during the routine pregame check must be corrected prior to kickoff, or the offending player(s) will not be allowed to enter the game." The policy also required players to be removed from games until in-game violations were corrected.

In other words, while the NFL has not announced plans to keep Williams off the field over his Caddix cleats, its existing rules provide a potential enforcement mechanism that goes well beyond simply issuing another fine.

While speaking to Rasmussen on the potential of the NFL barring Williams from playing because he refuses to cover the Caddix branding, he jokingly said, "I think it's a great idea. I think they should definitely bench Caleb Williams."

He also said, in response to a question about whether or not he believes Williams will continue to defy the league's demands, "He seems like a guy who's just does what he wants to do, and if he does, good for him. If he doesn't, I get it too."

As for a potential partnership with Caleb Williams and Caddix in the future, Rasmussen said, "I met Caleb one time, and I've talked to him a handful of times, but he and I never talked about a deal ever." 

"Right now they're gonna focus on playing the season, you know? This isn't a conversation that needs to be had right now. We have to figure out if Caleb is gonna keep getting penalized and all that stuff, but I mean, we'll see. I would love to have a deal with Caleb Williams."

As for now, Caddix is a disrupter in the football cleats market. As Rasmussen said, "Obviously we want to disrupt, but I've had a number of conversations with most of the big brands in the past 12 months and some of them have become friends of mine. I got congratulations a couple of times last week from a certain company and a few of their employees, and it was great to hear."

"We're not gonna do things a conventional way. We're just not, and if it's gonna come down to safety or money, we're gonna put the first one every time, I promise."

Mark this down as another great American story of an entrepreneur using the tools and freedoms this great country has to offer to make a huge splash in the most profitable league in American sports, on the back of free advertising from one of the league's and American sports, brightest stars.
