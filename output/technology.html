Anthropic says helping others is core to its mission. 3 philanthropy gurus say they'll believe it when they see it.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei.

Chris Ratcliffe/Bloomberg/Getty Images

  • Anthropic outlined several ways it plans to give back in its IPO filing this week.
  • They include reserved shares, cofounder pledges, donation matching, and stock incentives.
  • Philanthropy gurus said it's partly "reputation management," and how the money is used will be key.

Anthropic's IPO filing this week contained myriad revelations about the state of the AI firm's finances, as well as an eye-opening series of claims about its commitment to helping others.

We asked three experts in the world of philanthropy what they thought of these giving pledges. Their verdict: we'll see.

A culture of giving?

The company behind the AI chatbot Claude, which filed this week to go public in what could be the biggest IPO in history, laid out how it plans to give back and encourage its employees to do the same.

Anthropic's seven cofounders said they've pledged 80% of their personal stock in the startup to charitable causes, gifts that could together be worth $80 billion.

They also said they've earmarked 5% of Anthropic's equity for a "fund devoted to the public good," and plan to set aside another 2% for every sustained 50% rise in the company's stock price, with its IPO price as the base.

Moreover, they said that new employees are offered a donation match, meaning that for every share they pledge to give away to good causes, Anthropic creates an additional share and matches the donation.

Anthropic said that just over half of its employees have pledged at least some of their equity, including 70% of employees hired before May 2024, and nearly 50% of those hired after that date.

The company noted that 25% of the former group, who were offered up to a 50% match, pledged the full amount. That figure was nearly 30% for the latter group, who were offered a 25% match.

Anthropic also said it plans to issue Class E shares — which carry limited voting rights but have no economic value — to reward tenure and charity.

Employees stand to receive one share for every six months they stay at the company, and one Class E share for every normal share they pledge to donate, Anthropic said.

Anthropic didn't respond to a request for comment from Business Insider.

'Judge us on this'

Three philanthropy experts told Business Insider via email this week that Anthropic's giving plan is promising but represents only a first step.

"It's admirable, and it's well-designed," said Dr. Vandana Arcot, the founder and CEO of Komselj, a philanthropic strategy firm. She noted that "building it into the compensation structure makes generosity the default, not the exception."

Arcot said Anthropic's giving seemed to be grounded in an "ethic of accountability, not abundance."

She pointed to CEO Dario Amodei's January essay in which he said that people who get rich from AI have a responsibility to address its economic fallout.

Arcot also cited Anthropic's $200 million commitment this summer to fund research on preparing people for AI's economic impacts.

She said that valuing generosity "tends to help companies attract mission-driven talent and keep it, which is a business case on top of the moral one."

She added that "defining your giving in advance is a way of saying: judge us on this."

But Arcot said her full evaluation of Anthropic's efforts will depend on "who sits on the decision-making body, how recipients are chosen, and what gets reported."

Bella DeVaan, the director of the Charity Reform Initiative and deputy director of the Program on Inequality and the Common Good at the Institute for Policy Studies, said there's such "dire" wealth inequality in the US that "any efforts to center the public benefit are commendable."

However, she said they're "no substitute for structural reform," and "giving to the public benefit shouldn't be voluntary, but should be guaranteed by our tax system."

DeVaan also noted the charitable pledges by Anthropic's cofounders aren't legally binding and can be revoked. She said it was "concerning" to see that personal interest could trump public benefit, and added that it's "exactly why voluntary efforts alone won't be sufficient to rely on."

Hans Peter Schmitz, a professor of nonprofit leadership at North Carolina State University, said he applauded Anthropic for promoting generosity from the top down, but it struck him as "part of an intentional effort in managing public opinion."

He said that any positive publicity around Anthropic's giving could help differentiate it from competitors and counter bad news about AI. The nascent technology has proven contentious due to concerns about job losses, environmental damage, cybersecurity threats, and even danger to humans.

Schmitz said Anthropic appeared to be engaging in "reputational management," as "pledges are a relatively cheap 'signal' to be viewed in a positive light."

"Like many pledges in the past, there is also a high likelihood that it may never happen and/or a lot of the funding is channeled into a narrow set of issues, like AI safety," he said.

Canceled port calls and cash appeals: Inside the final months of the Villa Vie Odyssey's doomed round-the-world cruise

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
The Villa Vie Odyssey was bogged down by financial trouble in its final months at sea.

Suy SE/AFP/Getty Images

Villa Vie Residences promised residents the world. The company's cruise ship, the Odyssey, kept circling one corner of it while finances worsened, services deteriorated, and cash calls mounted.

"We were going in a smaller and smaller circle," said Claude Sadovsky, who joined the Odyssey when it first set sail from Belfast in September 2024. "I signed up for a world cruise, and I was in purgatory."

By early October, Sadovsky was frustrated enough with the itinerary changes that he left the ship and returned to his home in Maui. For Starr Davis, who also joined in Belfast in 2024, it was the constant appeals for fuel money that got her to jump ship in late September. "I got so sick of all the cash calls," she said.

For those aboard the Odyssey in its final months, the crisis grew so acute that some passengers began fundraising for crew members, residents told Business Insider.

The financial distress culminated in the ship's abrupt halt in Cambodia on Tuesday, when passengers were given eight hours to pack up their belongings and leave. While the speed of the end came as a shock to some, residents said signs of trouble had been growing.

"The ship was just a pressure cooker," Davis said. "You know, 'Are we going to exist?'"

In an interview with Business Insider on Friday, Villa Vie Residences cofounder and executive chairman Mikael Petterson confirmed the company had made cost-cutting changes. "There were itinerary changes," he said. "Of course there were."

He said the changes aboard the Odyssey — home to a "never-ending" cruise for permanent residents who bought cabins for $119,999 to $439,999 — were driven by rising fuel costs and logistical challenges.

"When you're the first one doing something, of course you're going to make a ton of mistakes," he said.

Petterson said roughly 88% of Villa Vie is owned by residents; the rest is owned by the founders, management team, and him. A drawback of that structure is that residents were the ones turned to when money got tight, he said: "When there's money needed, residents are naturally called upon because there's no private equity. There's no big owner."

"When things didn't go as planned, it was a double whammy," he added.

"That is what made Villa Vie Residences so strong, yet so weak."

Villa Vie Residences CEO Kathy Villalba did not respond to multiple requests for comment from Business Insider. In a now-deleted apology to residents, Villalba said Villa Vie was still speaking to potential investors and "holding onto whatever hope remains." Columbia Cruise Services, Villa Vie's operational partner, did not reply to several requests for comment.

The round-the-world route started shrinking

Villa Vie had marketed the three-and-a-half-year voyage as a circumnavigation with more than 425 port calls across 147 countries.

For the period between October 2024 and October 2026, its initial itinerary listed 275 ports in 69 countries and territories, or 54 sovereign nations.

As fuel costs rose after the onset of the war with Iran and Villa Vie's financial pressures worsened, the company increasingly pared down its route: It canceled stops, slowed the ship at sea, extended stays in ports, and revisited destinations to conserve fuel and reduce operating costs.

To see how plans changed, Business Insider compared itineraries the ship issued at four different times: its initial itinerary from October 2024; its November 2025 itinerary, which showed where it went in 2024 and 2025; one from January 2026 showing changes before the war; and an October 2026 itinerary showing this year's stops. We also reviewed messages Villalba and Petterson sent to ship residents at various points over the past three months.

Villa Vie Odyssey passengers in Cambodia after the voyage abruptly ended.

TANG CHHIN Sothy / AFP via Getty Images

We found that while the ship visited 54 nations between October 2024 and October 2026, it missed some territories and called at only 198 ports — 28% fewer than originally planned. Across the two years, our comparison of the itineraries showed the ship spent nearly two fewer months at sea than initially scheduled.

Petterson blamed the skyrocketing price of fuel: "We had no choice," he said.

Business Insider's analysis shows the Odyssey also made itinerary changes before fuel prices rose, including in Australia and New Zealand. Residents said those appeared to be for reasons other than fuel costs.

Planning around an ever-changing itinerary

For some residents, the route changes turned an around-the-world voyage into one long bout of déjà vu. "We have revisited some of the same ports countless times in the last several months," Angela Nuran Gokturk, 65, said.

Messages from Petterson and Villalba to residents demonstrated how fuel, financing, and operating costs directly shaped the ship's route.

In a message to residents on the ship's internal messaging platform on August 1, Villalba said they would cancel a stop in Brunei and sail directly from Singapore to Nha Trang in southern Vietnam "to conserve fuel and reduce operating costs."

On August 7, Petterson said in a memo that the ship had "aggressively reduced costs" and "redesigned itineraries to spend more time in port and consume less fuel."

In a message on September 6, Villalba said the Odyssey would extend its stay in Ha Long, Vietnam, by one or two days, depending on "fuel availability and funding." What was meant to be a three-night stay became a 10-night layover, the revised itinerary shows.

Residents said the route changes made it difficult to plan flights and excursions in advance. "People stopped trying to make plans because things could change literally within a few days," said Sally Stunkel, 75, who joined the ship in February.

During the stay in Ha Long, Stunkel joined an eight-person excursion to China, but the group had already booked flights to one of the Vietnamese stops that was later cut from the itinerary. They had to buy new tickets to rejoin the ship at its revised next port.

"We spent more money trying to chase the ship, basically, to the next port so we could then board again," she said.

The Villa Vie Odyssey had frequent itinerary changes.

Suy Se / AFP via Getty Images

Singapore became a repeat stop: The ship stopped there three times between May and July, spending 16 days there in total, according to the October 20 itinerary.

Stunkel said the one change that frustrated her most was the removal of Japan and South Korea from the itinerary. In March, the ship's public itinerary showed it was supposed to visit 19 ports in Japan and South Korea, between June 26 and August 19. In April, the itinerary was updated to instead show stops in Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam, and Brunei during that period.

Stunkel said she had bought a cabin on the cruise in part because it was scheduled to visit Japan over the summer. She said she was "extremely disappointed" by the change, adding, "Japan is my No. 1 bucket list destination."

Onboard services like dining and internet were hit

Some onboard services were also disrupted.

In a memo to residents on September 10, Villalba said that because the ship had fewer residents on board and fewer staff available, dining service would be consolidated. An on-board restaurant, The Palms, would temporarily close, while breakfast and lunch would be served buffet-style. Dinner would be a combination of à la carte and buffet. Davis said residents would joke when they went to dinner that it was "slim pickings."

Starr Davis said the final months on the ship were like a "pressure cooker."

Starr Davis

Internet and TV services also suffered. Two residents said the internet had become spotty and unreliable. In a September 7 memo, Villalba said she had paid an outstanding balance and that internet service was being restored.

Paul Montoya, 68, said the internet issues especially affected digital nomads living and working on the ship. Some would have to get off at port to find a nearby hotel with internet access, he said.

Davis said air conditioning on the ship was throttled up or down pending fuel needs, adding, "Everything was about saving fuel."

Petterson, who did not live on the ship and said he was not closely involved in its daily operations, said the company took measures to save money that went beyond conserving fuel.

"We were cutting back where the residents would feel it the least, and that balance sometimes was difficult," he said.

Residents were repeatedly asked to help keep the ship moving

Management made repeated requests for residents to help cover Villa Vie's costs.

In April, Villa Vie offered residents an "Emergency Loan Investment Opportunity," asking them to lend tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars to the company at interest rates of 12% to 24%, according to a letter viewed by Business Insider.

Petterson said the request netted around $2 million that helped the ship survive for a few months.

Aerial view of the Odyssey's pool deck.

Villa Vie Odyssey

From April onwards, Gokturk said it was "cash call after cash call."

Gokturk and her husband did not participate in the loan program, she said, but they contributed to later appeals, including a voluntary fuel surcharge. "It became exhausting because it was constantly, 'Hey, we need more money. We need more money. If we don't get the money, then we're not going to be able to sail,'" Gokturk said.

The uncertainty affected the mood aboard the ship. "It became very, very difficult," she said.

Stunkel said the cash calls felt "incessant." She continued, "We felt like they were just leaching us out of whatever we had to keep the ship moving until the last possible moment."

Four residents said that when they heard that staff had gone unpaid, they organized a fundraiser for the crew. Montoya said residents collected about $12,000 in cash outside the ship's café and distributed it to workers in $100 bills.

There were several times in the last couple of months when payment owed to the crew was delayed, Petterson said, including by three weeks this month. He said as of around Wednesday, the crew had been paid. An employee in the Florida office said staff had received a partial payment, and that commissions hadn't been paid in months.

Petterson said the company had been doing everything it could to keep the ship afloat. The company had tried to make the fuel surcharge mandatory but realized the residents' contracts didn't allow for it, so it became voluntary. Some residents contributed to the surcharge, while others bought additional discounted cabins through the VIP program.

He said he had spent July looking for outside investment. By August, the situation was dire. A potential deal with Oneil Khosa, the former CEO of Margaritaville's cruise line, fell through last week. They were out of time. Business Insider was unable to reach Khosa for comment.

In the days since the Odyssey's journey came to a halt in Cambodia, passengers have expressed a range of emotions. Some are heartbroken that their dream retirement came crashing to an end; others are hopeful the ship will sail again. One passenger said a group of fellow passengers is working on a "Hail Mary" attempt at buying the ship.

Petterson said the focus is on a recapitalization, adding, "Preferably we need an operational partner that can come in with a stronger hand and a bigger pocket."

Several residents said that despite how it ended, they don't regret the experience. But Sadovsky, who left before the Odyssey came to a halt, said the voyage he thought he had bought had become unrecognizable by the time he disembarked.

"There was no reason for me to stay because I wasn't being exposed to new places anymore," he said. "I was revisiting the same old places, and I'd rather be home."

Welcome to Marathon Sunday!

Yours truly will be among nearly 60,000 runners taking on the five boroughs today. I've spent months training for this one — with a little help from Muse, Meta's AI agent.

Several weeks ago, I wrote about how I had become addicted to Muse. Since then, Muse has become part of my marathon training, helping me map out long runs and shape an overall race plan. After the Staten Island half marathon last month, it even asked me, unprompted, "how'd the half go?"

The other day, I gave Muse my bib number and asked it to track my run. I'm hoping for real-time feedback and analysis, delivered straight through my AirPods, on how my race is unfolding and how I can stay on pace to achieve my goals.

But no AI agent can replicate the wisdom from real people.

At Business Insider, five of us are running the NYC marathon this year. We range from a first-time marathoner (Discourse editor Zak Jason) to a true veteran in Anthony DeMaio on the sales team, who will be running his 23rd NYC marathon (and roughly his 40th overall marathon; he says he's lost count!)

I asked them to share their best marathon advice for runners, spectators, or if you're simply trying to understand why someone would pay $330 to run 26.2 miles.

Here's what they had to say:

Noah Sheidlower, senior economy reporter: Live in the moment and acknowledge the spectators who came out to support. After I left Facebook, I started a wine business called Vinebase in 2020; we built a tech platform that used AI to help winemakers sell their wine online and manage the digital side of their business.

I use my playbooks and AI agents to replace additional staff

My sister and I started AMP, originally called The AI Exchange, before ChatGPT was released in November 2022, because I was using AI in Vinebase and saw how the technology was progressing. ChatGPT came out about a month later and started really accelerating the AI timeline, so we went all in.

We have four full-time employees and four part-time contractors right now, but we'd need double if we weren't running as much as we are on AI. Given the company's current size, we'd need at least four to five additional people, including a sales and marketing manager, customer support specialist, executive assistant, and operations coordinator.

The way we structure these systems is based on something we call playbooking. Every department in the company has its own system. Each system is a collection of playbooks with AI agents that can run independently or together.

How we build our AI playbooks

To build an AI playbook, pick one piece of work you do over and over, and write down what starts it, what goes into the task, and what should come out at the end. Next, write the steps the way you'd train a new hire: each step, the decisions in it, and what "good" looks like.

Start running it with AI, and while you watch, compare the result to how you would've done it. Then, fix the playbook, not the output, until you trust it to run without you.

I always think through how I want a playbook to work and outline it by hand. Whenever you're making an AI playbook, you want to own the thinking and the logic. Otherwise, you can fall into the trap of including something that you don't really understand, and then you're not going to be able to trust that system anyway.

We have an AI agent running our sales playbook to review customer calls and ensure they meet our standards, rather than having a sales manager review them manually. Also, instead of sales team members needing to ask their manager questions, they just ask the playbook, which then pulls the answers from our guide for that team.

There are two types of AI costs we have in the business. The first is the subscriptions to Claude and ChatGPT for every person on the team. The other is because the majority of our operations are these AI playbooks that are running in the background without us being the ones starting or triggering it. In September, we spent $8,510 on Claude Code to run our playbooks in the background, which is billed by usage and is separate from the team's monthly Claude and ChatGPT subscription costs.

Own the playbook, rent the tech

We say "own the playbook, rent the tech," which means that if you are creating really good playbooks for your AI to follow, it actually doesn't matter which tool you're running it in.

Right now, we have a setup that's running with Claude Code in the background. But I could run all of these playbooks in ChatGPT, Grok, Gemini, or Microsoft Copilot.

We try to avoid a lot of the risks that people are concerned about with AI going rogue by creating these detailed outlines. We don't just want the agents to run on vibes. There still needs to be a person to decide and design how the work gets done; AI just speeds up everything else. That's the job security we have in this AI era.

Start small, and get everyone involved

A few of us on the team who are more advanced with AI own the bigger, team-wide playbooks that the business depends on, but everyone playbooks their own personal workflow, and then we use that as the testing ground to graduate things into becoming more part of official systems.

My advice would be to just start with something small and really easy, because once you get your first win and see how AI can do that repetitive work for you, it's kind of like a snowball.

Your phone rings, and without thinking, you answer it. It's your spouse. Or your grandma. Maybe the friend you're always posting TikToks with online. And they're asking for your help.

Their voices have been cloned, and malicious actors are donning their audio likenesses to scam you out of your personal information, and sometimes cold, hard cash. That number is growing, making even more people susceptible to the deceptive tech, but there are very simple ways to protect yourself and your loved ones.  

Scroll through TikTok, and you'll come across hundreds of stories detailing tales concocted by scammers to impersonate your loved ones. Scams are on the rise. In 2025, Americans lost around $148.2 billion to digital crimes, a jump of more than 25 percent from the year before. Sales pitches for voice cloning services rose sevenfold in the last two years, reported NordVPN.

Why more people are getting fooled by audio scams 

One reason that number keeps ticking up is the proliferation of sophisticated — but more importantly accessible — generative AI tools, explained Marijus Briedis, CTO at NordVPN. 

"Some years ago, a cloned voice had something slightly off about it, and most people could hear it. That is gone now," Briedis said. 

Advanced technology is only one part of the growing scam industry, however. Briedis explained that such tools are being increasingly packaged, priced, and sold directly to the Average Joe. 

Voice cloning tools, previously a standalone novelty tech, are now sold as a single feature in all-encompassing, common criminal toolkits, or "scam kits," experts told Mashable. These kits include access to legitimate generative AI tools via jailbroken accounts, troves of personal data, and other features sold at increasingly affordable prices.

For example, a recent study of dark web marketplaces conducted by NordVPN found that a hacked ElevenLabs Scale account — complete with unlimited voice cloning — sold for just $150. An ElevenLabs API key was going for a mere $8.

Scam networks no longer need technologically savvy hackers or technologists. Just about anyone with a computer can do it. And that means you have to be prepared. 

Step 1: Know the threat and make a plan 

"When anyone can run the attack, volume becomes the strategy," Briedis said. "No single attempt has to be perfect. There just have to be a lot of them."

But asking someone to be on alert all the time is unfair, Briedis said. Up until now, the mantra has been: "Every message, every video, every call. Get it wrong once and that is the one that counts," Briedis explained. "Nobody holds that standard across a lifetime, and most security advice assumes they will."

As an alternative, users can prepare themselves, and their community, to be able to step up when the moment calls.

Prepare your contacts

Eyal Elazar, head of market Intelligence for online merchant security business Riskified, described effective scam preparation as being a "digital ambassador" for your inner circle. 

It begins with a little research. Find and save verified contact information for important callers, such as your bank, insurance company, or family lawyers. You can use this to cross reference callers later.

Then, talk to your circles about what a legitimate phone call would look like. Know who lists you as an emergency contact, and in what contexts. Are you a beneficiary on an account, and, if so, what do you need to know?

Set a code word

Most experts Mashable spoke to recommend creating a code word that you can use with friends and family to confirm who they are. Don't share this anywhere online. "Sounds silly right up until the day it does not," Briedis said.

Filter out the red flags  

There are also common scam requests that are instant red flags, experts told Mashable. Any demand for money over the phone is suspicious. There are more specific tells, too: 

  • Requests to read a code, sent via text or email, over the phone

  • Requests for passwords or other log-in credentials 

  • Demands for payment via gift cards, wire transfers, crypto exchanges, and payment apps

  • Suggestions to initiate a wire transfer over a voice note, a phone call, or a voicemail

  • Demands of ransom

  • Law enforcement dealings conducted via phone call

Step 2: Putting down the phone can stop a scammer.

"AI isn’t inventing new scams, but it's removing the tells we’ve learned to look for in the old ones," said Donnie Hasseltine, chief information security officer at Arkstone Defense. 

It can be hard to discern the small audio tells that make your loved ones' voice truly theirs, especially when an individual is up against social engineering that forces urgency and stirs panic. 

So, the next piece of advice is to hang up on your friends or "friends." 

Yes, this may cause some drama in the group chat. But if you can't always tell when a voice is real or cloned, it's best to cut them off and reset. 

If someone you personally know was supposedly on the other line, call them back on the number you're used to before agreeing to anything. If it's a bank or your workplace, call a listed number on an official website, the back of a credit card, or a piece of mail. Many institutions are already keenly aware of common phone scams that target customers. 

"Stop judging the content and change the channel instead," Breidis urged.

Step 3: Slow down.

The last, and most simple, piece of advice is to take a breath. Experts told Mashable that urgency is the number one red flag that may indicate you are the target of a scam, e.g., someone claiming to be from the electricity company demanding money immediately in exchange for keeping your power on. That supposed urgency also sets you up to be more susceptible to scammers. 

Andrew Jones, co-founder of Adaptive Security, said that a manufactured sense of urgency as well as a demand for secrecy are the two factors found in nearly every successful scam he has studied. 

"Nothing is so urgent that it can’t wait for you to verify it," Hasseltine agreed. 

So don't give in to the rush. Slow down, hang up the phone, and ask someone else to weigh in. 

And it bears repeating: Never. Send. Cash.
