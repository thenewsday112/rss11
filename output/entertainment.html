Copperfield! The New Musical review: A rollicking, big-hearted Dickens adaptation that is not to be missed

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Originally published as a serial, starting in 1849, Charles Dickens' The Personal History, Adventures, Experience and Observation of David Copperfield the Younger of Blunderstone Rookery is a tale rife with tragedy. But recent adaptations like Armando Iannucci's 2019 movie The Personal History of David Copperfield and Jeffrey Scharf's Copperfield! The New Musical focus on the hope and happiness found in a life story rocked repeatedly by woe.

However, for those taking notes on such things, Copperfield! does hew more closely to the events of Dickens' story, embracing some of its darkest moments without reserve. And yet, this new musical, now playing Off-Broadway, is a rapturous delight — perhaps in part because it doesn't shy away from its hero's hardships.

Copperfield! A New Musical is a classic story told with a raucous and sensationally talented ensemble.

Amelia Cormack, Jed Feder, Alexander Sovronsky, Henry Witcher, Cathryn Wake, and Nancy O'Connor in "Copperfield! The New Musical." Credit: Jeremy Daniel

With book, music, and lyrics by Scharf, Copperfield! is set across England, from 1835 to 1870. Directed and choreographed by Gabriel Barre (with associate director and choreographer Ashley Wren Collins), this musical kicks off with a grown David Copperfield (Bobby Conte) reflecting on his life. A dashing man, he ushers audiences back to the day of his birth, introducing his widowed mother Clara (Cathryn Wake) in fervent labor, while her devoted maid Peggotty (Amelia Cormack) assists, and his eccentric aunt Betsey Trotwood (Kara Mikula) flutters about making bizarre predictions and demands.

With an energetic song called "Betsey Trotwood Copperfield/It's a Boy," Mikula kicks the story off with rousing energy and plenty of spunk. Then comes Young David (Calvin Knegten at the performance I saw), who shoulders much of the first act, with his older counterpart stepping in on occasion to make observations or lend reflective moral support. Young David will be bounced on his mother's knee, then he's off to a seaside village to visit with Peggotty's charming family, before being sent to a cruel boarding school at the behest of his vicious stepfather, Mr. Murdstone (Will Reynolds).

From there, the poor boy will be orphaned then forced into hard labor, before seeking salvation from the aforementioned aunt. Even as he finds relief from one vexation, other troubles arise. But so, too, do meaningful relationships with debonair classmate Steerforth (Owen Squire Smith), scrappy debtor Mr. Micawber (Andy Taylor), a befuddled but brilliant family friend Mr. Dick (Aaron Michael Ray), and his benevolent bestie Agnes Wickfield (Tatiana Wechsler).

Now, as the story roams from the Copperfield estate to Yarmouth, London, and beyond across decades, Copperfield! keeps its cast almost alarmingly small. Around Conte's strong central performance, 13 adult actors not only play several characters each, but also pick up instruments to join in the orchestration. In this way, they turn a tiny stage — enhanced by flanking platforms and a modest but efficient set design — into a wide world that is cruel, kind, and often wondrous.

Bobby Conte leads a marvelous ensemble cast in Copperfield!

Bobby Conte and Cathryn Wake in "Copperfield! The New Musical." Credit: Jeremy Daniel

As an admirer of Iannucci's adaptation of David Copperfield, I thought of it often while witnessing Copperfield! In that, I found a curious distinction. Iannucci focused on David's pursuit of identity, drawing attention to how he was forced to code-switch and even change his name depending on his company. Scharf's approach is less cerebral, focusing instead on the search for home.

The plays opening number "I Am Home" is reprised throughout the show, as David reflects on when he felt safe and thought his happy ending was assured. Of course, whether you know Dickens' work or just that this musical is unlikely to end in the first act, this reprise toys with our hearts, because we know there's more to come. While the company brings out guitars, trumpets, pianos, drums, and more instrumentation, Scharf's songs range from dastardly ("Mr. Murdstone Is the Master Now") to playful ("I Shake Your Hand Sir") to mournful ("Always the Pleasure Was Mine") to rollicking ("I Am a Rover"). In this, Scharf creates a thrilling odyssey in a simple setting, using his music and relying on this sensational cast to build David's world with their unbridled vigor.

Tatiana Wechsler, Aaron Michael Ray, Bobby Conte, and Kara Mikula in "Copperfield! The New Musical." Credit: Jeremy Daniel

While the whole of the cast sings their hearts out, there are a few standouts. Cormack, who plays Peggotty and Mrs. Micawber, among others, bursts with warmth and comedic bounciness, so her every entrance is a blessing. Mikula, as Aunt Betsey, is a deranged delight, singing with the trilling enthusiasm and the force of a windstorm, to the point where I yearned to see her tackle Sweeney Todd as Mrs. Lovett. Alexander Sovronsky awed me with the sheer breadth of his characters, playing the effusively warm Mr. Peggotty, the malevolent Mr. Creakle, the dignified then drunken Mr. Quinion, and a smattering of bit parts aside. With the toss of his lush silver hair and a switch up of his mouth, Sovronsky needs no costume change to change character. And in group numbers, he is so clearly enthralled, whether singing, dancing, or playing an instrument, that he's hard to look away from.

Conte is strong in the lead role, delivering nuance and heartache whether mourning over his mother or falling in love, not once but twice. Wechsler is lovely in the role of besotted Agnes, while Wake is hilarious and surprising as David's first love, Dora. Playing his mother, Wake is serene and oft on the verge of tears, bursting with motherly adoration of him. As his sweet, giddy bride, she is unhinged in the best way. Watching her throw herself into the Olympian vocal back-and-forth of their courtship ("Do-o-or-a, Part One and Part Two"), I was reminded of Jane Krakowski and her recent Broadway stint in Oh, Mary! In this latter role Wake is explosively hilarious, deepening my appreciation for the sincerity in her first-act performance of Clara.

With so much going on, Copperfield! is a whole lot of show. And admittedly, that means the third act drags as the eponymous hero catches up to the romantic realization even newcomers to the story will clock in Act II. Still, bursting with energy, passion, and jaw-dropping talent, Copperfield! The New Musical is a wonder not to be missed.

Copperfield! The New Musical is now playing through Dec. 6 in New York.

The Curious Case of Benjamin Button review: The real mystery of this musical is why

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Perhaps when you think of The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, you think of Brad Pitt, who headlined David Fincher's 2008 film adaptation to the tune of 13 Oscar nominations (though no major category wins). That movie took major liberties with the 1922 F. Scott Fitzgerald short story on which it's based. So does the Olivier Award-winning new musical, which has just opened Off-Broadway at the Public Theater.

And that's for the best, as Fitzgerald's short story was mean and odd. Alas, the movie and musical made from it are less of both, and yet, I yearn for more.

What remains the same across all three iterations is this: Benjamin Button is born old and ages in reverse. Thus, his death is basically going from zygote to nothingness.

In the case of the musical, writer/lyricist Jethro Compton and composer/lyricist Darren Clark transport the story from 1860s Baltimore to a Cornish fishing village at the end of World War I. There, a quirky village consists of a baker with a wandering eye, a drunken tea shop proprietor, a woman who dreams of growing her lawn gnome into a husband (stranger things have happened), and Roger Button (Brandon J. Ellis), an expectant father who is not expecting to find his newborn son is an old man with a cap and cane.

Andrew Durand stars as Benjamin Button.

Andrew Durand and Kelsee Kimmel (foreground) with the company of "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button." Credit: Joan Marcus

Earlier this year, Durand played Brad Majors in Broadway's Rocky Horror Show revival. In The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, he thrusts his jaw forward and stoops his shoulders to convey that the eponymous hero is effectively a septuagenarian at birth. Of course, as Benjamin ages in reverse, Durand will be tasked with shifting his physicality to that of a man in his fifties, who is besotted by a twentysomething barmaid, Elowen Keene (Kelsee Kimmel). As she grows older, he'll grow more spry and lively, dancing and frolicking with children, then running away like one.

His performance is broad and cartoonish, framing the musical as a farce. Around him, the ensemble, who play multiple supporting characters along with instruments throughout, match this tone with relish. The music by Clark is rollicking, like a country fair. "Rag Ty Yw Tre" opens the show, setting up a theme of time and tide being fickle friends to man. Other songs will invoke nature too, like "The Western Wind," "The Kraken's Lullaby," and "The Tide Is Comin' In."

While the musical has garnered praise since its Off-West End debut to its West End run to its current Off-Broadway run at the Public, I struggled to make sense of the fervor for it. The orchestration was loud and muddled; I struggled to make out the lyrics outside of the chorus. The voices of the company are melodic, and their playing enthusiastic, yet neither can distract that many of Clark and Compton's songs are not just reprisals, but repetitive. "Time is a funny thing" is a sentiment repeated over and over, making the two-and-a-half-hour production feel longer and tiresome.

Benjamin Button is a bastard.

The company of "The Curious Case of Benjamin Button." Credit: Joan Marcus

This is true to the Fitzgerald story too. In both, Benjamin marries a younger woman, and then flees her as she grows old and he grows young. Compton and Clark's musical offers a different motivation than Fitzgerald's, but their revision makes his trespass even crueler. And disturbingly, they doesn't seem to realize how much of a cad their hero is. Or else they give him a maudlin backstory as justification.

In the short story, Benjamin's father urges him to play with other children. In this musical, his mother and father lock him in the attic, ashamed of his bizarre existence. After years of such neglect, Benjamin takes to sneaking out on Friday nights to a pub to experience community and beer. There, he meets his future bride, though much drama will come before they wed. Then, tragedy will strike their family, and he'll flee.

It's not that The Curious Case of Benjamin Button doesn't recognize that running away from your wife in a time of shared heartbreak isn't horrid. But rather that the musical's rush to forgive him is not just alarming but repulsive. Opposite Durand, Kimmel offers a performance that is charming and tender, while punctuated with occasional comedic bravado to keep in the musical's overall tone. But Elowen is a character thin as tissue paper, who exists purely to satisfy Benjamin's every need, be it love, forgiveness, reflection, or beer. Yet I felt for her as her love runs off on a vague journey of self-improvement that is a circle.

This dreadful second act lumbers into a confounding climax. Songs haphazardly tie up the loose ends of every clumsy thread of character arc, from horny baker to wannabe gnome wife. Flimsy characters flit around as Benjamin is treated as some profound figure, while his behavior is generally that of a fuckboy. By the end, I had the lyric "It's all just a matter of time" ringing in my head, as I felt I'd wasted mine.

The Curious Case of Benjamin Button is now playing at the Public Theater until Dec. 20.

Gracie Abrams Goes From Juliet to Romeo During ‘SNL’ Halloween Performances

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Gracie Abrams turned her Saturday Night Live return into a two-part costume change, dressing as both leads from Baz Luhrmann’s Romeo + Juliet across her performances on the show’s Halloween episode Saturday (Oct. 31).

For her first song, “Hit the Wall” from her third album Daughter from Hell, Abrams wore Claire Danes’ angel costume from the 1996 film and opened the performance from behind an aquarium, a nod to the movie’s fish-tank meeting scene. She returned later in the episode for “Here You Come, There You Go,” a track from the album’s deluxe edition, in Leonardo DiCaprio’s Hawaiian shirt paired with the metal sleeves from his knight’s armor.

It was Abrams’ second turn as SNL musical guest, following her December 2024 debut on an episode hosted by Chris Rock. Inde Navarrette hosted Saturday’s episode, her first.

The appearance comes off the biggest commercial year of Abrams’ career. Daughter from Hell, released July 17 via Interscope and produced with longtime collaborator Aaron Dessner, debuted at No. 1 on the Billboard 200 with 124,000 equivalent album units, her first chart-topper on the all-genre list. Her 2024 album The Secret of Us had peaked at No. 2.

“Hit the Wall” was the album’s lead single, and Abrams has said it set the tone for everything that followed. “I just remember it being done,” she told Billboard of writing it, describing the session as almost trance-like. The song reached the Billboard Hot 100 and No. 45 on the Billboard Global 200. Her highest-charting song to date remains “That’s So True,” the deluxe-edition track from The Secret of Us that reached No. 6 on the Hot 100 and became her first top 10 there.

Abrams takes Daughter from Hell on the road in December. Her Look at My Life Tour opens Dec. 2 in Denver and runs into May 2027, including four nights at London’s O2 Arena.
