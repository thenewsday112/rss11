At 27, I quit my dream job without a plan. It killed me at the time, but paid off over a decade later.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
As much as I loved working my dream job, I couldn't help feeling like I needed to try something new.

Courtesy of Mikkel Woodruff

  • I left my dream job at 27 without a new one lined up. I had no idea what would come next.
  • By relying on my network, I found a new role and eventually pivoted to running my own business.
  • It's been over 15 years since I took that first risk by leaving, and I'm so grateful I did it.

When I started my dream job, I never imagined walking away from it just two years later.

After all, my long-held fantasy of working in theme-park design had lived up to the hype: I collaborated with talented people every day, got to play in the parks for free whenever I wanted, and learned so many industry secrets about making the magic happen. I'd even gotten to help design an internationally acclaimed project.

It seemed nuts to leave, but I made up my mind when I realized I'd started spending more time fantasizing about what I could be doing elsewhere than I did getting excited about what was happening inside the office I drove to every day.

I wasn't confident in my decision, but there's no joy in floundering either, so I felt I had to commit one way or another. It was scary, and I didn't have another job lined up or a concrete plan when I decided to resign, but deep down, I knew I was ready for something new.

By relying on my network of friends and family, I was able to make my next move

As much as I loved my work in theme-park design, I felt ready for a new adventure.

Courtesy of Mikkel Woodruff

While I figured out what was next for me, my parents graciously agreed to let me move back into their house in the suburbs of Long Island.

As much as I would miss Orlando, I appreciated this small safety net.

And with no job lined up or savings to rely on, I started leaning on my network of friends and family in New York. I told anyone I could think of that I needed a job, and I was up for the adventure of trying something new.

Luckily, thanks to friends and my belief that closed mouths don't get fed (which has proven true time and again), I found an incredible opportunity at a then-startup in New York City called Rent the Runway.

The new position was different from my previous work, but it sounded exciting and relied on skills I had really strengthened in Orlando.

My parents drove me to the train station every weekday so I could commute into the office, and I saved enough money to pay off some of my college debt and move into an NYC apartment within the year.

In time, I learned to trust my gut with big career moves.

Courtesy of Mikkel Woodruff

After about two and a half years at that job, though, a familiar feeling had returned. My focus kept drifting to new dreams and worthwhile opportunities outside the office walls, and I felt a magnetic pull toward them.

While working at Rent the Runway, I'd been building my own photography business on the side. It had started to take off, and even though it would be risky, it felt like the right time to take a step toward becoming a business owner and solopreneur.

This time around, though, I wasn't so afraid to take a leap. After all, it wasn't too long ago that I had first pushed through that fear and felt risks pay off.

Years later, I'm glad I trusted my gut and embraced a more unconventional career path

Now, I have a career I never would've built without taking risks.

Courtesy of Mikkel Woodruff

Looking back, I still can't believe I left a job voluntarily without a clear plan, not once, but twice. Truthfully, it felt out of character.

Growing up, my parents wouldn't even let me quit piano lessons that I didn't find much joy in. "You stick with it until the year is over," they'd tell me. Even if I didn't feel like going to a friend's house for a playdate as a kid, they would push me to go, saying, "We stick to our commitments, Mikkel."

As I grew up, I began to understand the method behind how my parents raised me. Their morals became my values: When you commit to something, you stick with it.

But those piano lessons had an end date and time, and so did those playdates. Accepting a job is a commitment, too, but most roles have no clear end.

How do you know if you should stay at a job or when it's time to leave — especially if it's one you love? Now that I've navigated that unknown territory, I know it can come down to trusting your gut.

And over time, I've realized the real dream is living a life that excites me and lets me stay true to myself.

Now that I'm older, my priorities and goals feel more clear.

Courtesy of Mikkel Woodruff

It's been about 15 years since I left that first role. My path in life has been windier than expected, but it's been fantastic.

I ended up moving from from New York City to North Carolina, meeting my now-husband, and traveling the world with him. We both work for ourselves and I've built a career I'm proud of, one that I'd never have had without the risks I'd taken years prior.

And you know what's funny? I'm now even back to living in Florida, in a city just north of Orlando.

Ukraine is pushing fiber-optic drones to greater ranges. One maker questions whether that's worth the trade-offs.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Making Ukraine's fiber-optic drones fly further comes with trade-offs that might not be worth it, Ukrainian drone maker TechEx said.

Pavlo Bahmut/Ukrinform/Sipa USA via Reuters Connect

  • Ukraine's jam-resistant fiber-optic drones have been flying farther than ever.
  • But one maker questions whether more range is worth sacrificing payload capacity.
  • Longer cables add weight, potentially making some drones less useful in combat.

As companies continue to innovate, Ukraine's unjammable fiber-optic drones are flying farther and farther, but one company is questioning whether it's worth continuing to extend their range.

These drones, which can transmit commands and video through a physical cable, are not affected by enemy electronic warfare, specifically conventional radio-frequency jamming. Providing clearer video feeds than other FPV drones, they're useful for both surveillance and attack, and Ukrainian officials and companies have celebrated as their ranges have increased.

But TechEx, a major Ukrainian fiber-optic and interceptor-drone maker, told Business Insider that pushing those limits further may not be worth the trade-offs.

The company has built models with ranges of up to 35 and 40 kilometers. "We are currently considering whether it's still necessary to do the next versions of the fiber optic drones to fly, let's say, 45 or 50 kilometers," said Oleg, a TechEx executive who asked to be identified only by his first name for security reasons.

The reason behind the hesitation is that flying farther requires a heavier spool, as well as larger batteries to keep the added spool weight aloft, Oleg said. "This may affect the amount of the payloads carried by the drone."

"So we are still figuring out whether it's something that will be actually used on the front lines to do that long-range FPVs, or we will stick to, let's say, 40 or 45 kilometers."

Drone companies are constantly having to balance the weight of the cameras, explosives, weaponry attachments, communications gear, and other equipment. That balance is harder for fiber-optic drone makers to maintain because their drones are also carrying a spool of cable.

The fiber-optic cable and its spool add weight to fiber-optic drones that units and makers need to consider.

Jose Colon/Anadolu via Getty Images

Some Ukrainian drone makers have also said that shorter ranges are more effective anyway for fiber-optic drones because longer distances increase the risk of the cable breaking.

Describing TechEx's Stalker XO-10 and Stalker XO-15 drones, Ukraine's state-backed innovation platform Brave1 said the "fiber-optic drones have some limitations, including the need to carry a coil of cable that gradually unwinds during flight and provides communication with the operator."

"Since the coil itself has some weight, this can limit the payload of the UAV," it said, referring to uncrewed aerial vehicles.

Brave1 said that TechEx has so far been able to "mitigate this limitation" of the cable weight "through good engineering solutions" and assessed its work "allows these drones to achieve high payloads while maintaining the other advantages of fiber optics."

Whether that would continue to be true at longer ranges is a question mark right now.

Olha, a TechEx representative, told Business Insider that the company developed technology to make the spool lighter, making "tiny tweaks and changes" to how it's made and how the fiber is wound onto it. Ukraine's Ministry of Defense said last year that the Stalker series drones have a "record-low spool weight among Ukrainian fiber-optic drones."

Not all companies see the weight-to-range payoff the same way, and that could be good for Ukraine. Units use a host of drones from different companies for different missions, and soldiers say they like the flexibility to choose the equipment best suited to each mission. Some missions may call for longer-range drones carrying smaller payloads, while others require more firepower over shorter distances.

Fiber-optic drones come with big advantages, but also limitations.

Jose Colon/Anadolu via Getty Images

And attack missions aren't the only ones where explosive weight is a factor. Soldiers have described using fiber-optic drones for intelligence gathering with their onboard cameras. But there can still be a trade-off, with some higher-quality cameras weighing more.

Fiber-optic drones are just one part of Ukraine's growing arsenal, which includes long-range strike drones, mid-strike drones, and even heavy bombers. Many of these are able to carry much heavier payloads than small FPV drones. Each system comes with advantages and vulnerabilities, forcing manufacturers and soldiers to weigh which capabilities matter most for a given mission.

TechEx receives constant feedback and requests from the units that use its drones, which inform its decisions about what to build.

It has a technical support department, which Oleg says works with more than 100 units. It results in weekly changes to products ranging from small updates to major alterations.

Feedback, along with field testing by military pilots and operators, is critical to the work the company does, and has led it toward counterintuitive approaches, away from what it had initially thought would work best and would be what units want.

For instance, the prototype of the company's Striker interceptor drone was originally much bigger, with a 1.5-kilogram payload, but testing and feedback showed it needed to be smaller and more agile and didn't need such a large payload.

"We were able to completely redesign, and let's say eventually it looked nothing like the initial prototype," Olha said. Developing new weapons of war is a constant process of reevaluating, rethinking, and evolving.

A 'humbling' career move led to Catherine Zeta-Jones' big break

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Catherine Zeta-Jones currently stars in "Killing Jackie."

Getty; TriStar Pictures; USA Films; BI

Catherine Zeta-Jones' arrival in Hollywood in the mid-1990s was an ego check.

The Welsh actor, then in her 20s, had starred for years on the popular early-'90s British series "The Darling Buds of May," and was already a sensation in the UK. But when she touched down in California, she went from being recognized on the street to having casting agents ask her in auditions if she had any acting experience.

That wasn't all.

"I couldn't get cable TV," Zeta-Jones told Business Insider with a laugh. "I had been working since I was 9, but I couldn't buy anything in the States because I didn't have a credit card yet. I had to start from scratch."

Starting over turned out to be worth it for Zeta-Jones. After taking a role in the CBS miniseries "Titanic," she caught the attention of Steven Spielberg, who recommended her for "The Mask of Zorro." The role would make her a star Stateside and lead to an illustrious three-decade career in Hollywood.

Business Insider's "Role Play" series features interviews where acting legends revisit their most memorable roles. See more here.

Zeta-Jones is now known on both sides of the pond for memorable roles ranging from the pregnant wife of a Mexican drug lord in "Traffic" to her Oscar-winning turn as the iconic Velma Kelly in "Chicago" to Morticia Addams in Netflix's "Wednesday."

But despite her long list of credits, there's one thing she still hadn't done for a role until now: use her native Welsh accent.

Catherine Zeta-Jones in "Killing Jackie."

AMC+

Her latest project, the AMC+ series "Killing Jackie," marks Zeta-Jones' long-awaited return to her Welsh roots. After decades of having her brain work overtime doing English, American, and Scottish accents — and many more — the opportunity to speak in her native accent was grounding. "It was liberating and cathartic," Zeta-Jones said.

So, too, was the subject matter. Based on the 2018 novel "The Price You Pay" by Nick Harkaway (using the pseudonym Aidan Truhen), the revenge thriller stars Zeta-Jones as the titular Jackie Price, a Welsh high-end art dealer with a shady past in the drug trade who discovers a hit has been put out on her. With a deadly team of assassins known as The Seven Demons on her tail, Jackie goes to great lengths to stay alive — at one point even biting off a man's ear.

Navigating the series' ultra-violent scenes was a blast for Zeta-Jones, who relished the chance to go off the rails.

"I love this character," Zeta-Jones said. "I wanted the audience to come along on this ride with me and root for her. In all the craziness."

In the latest edition of Business Insider's Role Play series, Zeta-Jones reflects on her instant bond with Sean Connery, helping her husband Michael Douglas get a part in "Traffic," and the leading man she was most intimidated to work across from.

Zeta-Jones in 1997.

Frank Trapper/Corbis/Getty Images

On being discovered by Steven Spielberg and golfing with Sean Connery

Business Insider: Let's go back to your 20s. You're in London, where you can't walk down the street without paparazzi all over you thanks to "The Darling Buds of May." Then you go to LA, where nobody knows you. What was that like?

Catherine Zeta-Jones: I had six months on a visa, so I could go and give the States a shot for six months. I would have hated myself if I decided the American thing wasn't for me. I would have always thought, What if I had given it a shot?

It was good for me once I got over my ego being knocked a bit. I'd been in this business since I was 9 years old, professionally in theater and in television, and a few films in Britain at that point. But I'd go into casting agents, and they'd be asking me what I had done.

I took a job that was not a huge career leap for me — the CBS show, "Titanic," the TV series after the blockbuster movie came out. I kept reminding myself that I came to give America a shot.

Steven Spielberg, on a Sunday night, was watching this CBS "Titanic" series when he saw me. As the producer of "The Mask of Zorro," he suggested that I screen test. Literally, within a week of it coming on TV, I was shipped down to Mexico to screen test with Antonio Banderas. Five days later, they called my agent and said I got the job, and three days after that, I was down there starting my sword-fighting lessons. 

After that, you worked across from Sean Connery in "Entrapment," which cemented your stardom. Is it true you got that role because you told Connery you play golf?

[Laughs.] He used to say that tongue-in-cheek. You know, Sean Connery was a dear, dear force of nature. 

Would you guys play golf?

Yeah. 

Did you ever beat him?

No. And even if I could, I would never tell. I beat Michael Douglas. Any day of the week. But not Sean Connery.

You can't beat 007. 

No way. Even if he cheated, I would just turn my head. 

Zeta-Jones and Michael Douglas in 1999.

Ron Galella/Ron Galella Collection/Getty Images

On working with her husband on 'Traffic' and tap dancing with Christopher Walken

Speaking of your husband, is it true that Michael finally signed on to "Traffic" because he kept looking over your shoulder, looking at the drafts you were getting for the role you took in that movie?

There is a lot of truth in that. I was pregnant, and no one knew. And Steven Soderbergh asked me to do "Traffic." And I kept saying, "Oh my gosh, there's no way I can do this. Steven Soderbergh wants me in his movie, and I'm having a baby!" I was happy to be having a baby, but I so wanted to do this movie. So I called Steven and said, "I love the script, um, I think she would be even better if she was pregnant." And he said, "When are you due?" I went, "August," and he said, "We'll make it work." 

Michael never reads my scripts. I never read Michael's scripts. It's the one thing we kind of do on our own; we don't really talk shop at home. We just don't. But he knew how great that script was, and so Soderbergh called him and asked him if he had a chance to read my script. [Laughs.] And he said, "Kinda, but she's been telling me." So that's when he came on board. 

What I loved about that movie was not just the stellar performances, but I love that I can say I've been in a movie with Michael Douglas. Because if he wasn't my husband, I so would want to work with him. So even though we didn't have any scenes together, it was phenomenal. 

You starred in the rom-com "America's Sweethearts." The movie ends with Christopher Walken showing up to the junket to show footage of the movie he made with your character. It turns out to be all behind-the-scenes footage of the movie, which seems to be a musical, and we see you tap dancing with Walken. Is it true that subplot is in "America's Sweethearts" because you specifically wanted to tap dance with Christopher Walken?

Absolutely. I wanted to tap dance with Christopher Walken! I'm a big tap dancer. There are not many actors of my generation or Michael's generation, yes, [Michael Douglas' father] Kirk's generation, that can tap dance. So when I had the chance to do that, I was like, "Guys, we can't miss this chance." It was totally, selfishly, for myself. 

Zeta-Jones and Tom Hanks in "The Terminal."

DreamWorks

We touched on a few leading men you have worked with over your career. Looking back, is there one who made you stay on your game the most? Someone who you knew you had to bring your A-game to every time you walked on set?

I have to say Sean, but it wasn't that I had to be on my A-game. I just never wanted to stop acting with him, because we were such good buddies. He was so incredibly protective of me, like a daughter he never had, and he said that to me. And I loved his wife. I can't really say family, because he's not family, but I never wanted to act with anyone else after Sean Connery. I just didn't. It was fun. 

But I will say when I did "The Terminal" with Steven Spielberg at the helm and working so closely with Tom Hanks, I did go to work on the first day and go, "Mommy, I want to go home and cuddle." I got a little intimidated. Then — and I say this all the time — I learned the better they are, the nicer they are.

Now I've worked for so long, I've worked with some actors and gone, "Ugh, you just burst my bubble; I so wanted to work with you." Very rarely, but it has happened.

But working with all those guys on the "Oceans" movies: Matt [Damon], Brad [Pitt], George [Clooney]… I did a movie with George with the Coen brothers, and that was exceptional. So it's not so much bringing your A-game, because I want to do that with everyone I work across from.

Even on the movies where you realize, I don't think this is going to work, how do you get through it and feel positive and 100% committed? It's usually that the actors you're working with are so amazing.

My family and I moved back home to Kentucky from Los Angeles. It gave me more time with my dad before his sudden death.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
The author is glad her son got more time with his grandfather.

Courtesy of Tonilyn Hornung

  • My husband and I moved back to Kentucky from Los Angeles eight years ago.
  • I wanted to be closer to family, and for my son to get more time with his grandparents.
  • When my dad suddenly passed away four years ago, I was even more grateful for that time.

Eight years ago, my husband and I decided to move our family from California to Kentucky. On days we miss our LA favorites, like animal fries from In-N-Out Burger or warm sunshine when it's dreary and 22 degrees, we play the game: What would life be like if we stayed? It starts out breezy with talk of Disneyland rides and running into celebrities like Harry Connick Jr., but the conversation never lasts too long.

"If we stayed," I say softly, "I would've missed out on time with my dad."

When we lived on the other side of the country, we had less time together

That's when the fun stops, and my husband reaches for my hand. We spent our first four years in Louisville living 15 minutes away from my parents, and then my father died unexpectedly. If we had stayed in LA, family time would've been reduced to two weeks a year and short phone calls. I couldn't have predicted that coming back home would give us such a gift — more time with my dad. And it almost didn't happen.

Committing to a cross-country move was no small thing. This was why I hadn't packed up our house in the middle of a no-sleeping week during my son's colicky fourth trimester. I was ready for family dinners, sleepovers, and the support I'd craved since our kiddo was born. But even though I felt ready, my husband needed time. A big move meant big changes in careers and friends. So, we waited and considered while our kid moved out of diapers and into Pull-Ups.

I continued calling and FaceTiming my parents so I could feel more connected. And this is how our nightly story time chats became a ritual — until my son started asking to see his grandparents during the day, too.

One particular afternoon, my toddler said, "Call Papa?" So I phoned my dad at work because he always answered. After talking to his grandson about Legos, we finalized a time for an upcoming visit. That's when I confided that I was nervous that a move home wasn't in our future. With more confidence in me than I had in myself, he said gently, "You'll know when it's right." Since my dad was, you know…my dad, I believed him.

We decided to move back to Kentucky at the right time

Five years after our son was born and many discussions later, my husband and I finally found the right time — just before our son entered Kindergarten. My dad was right. Once we were back in Louisville, it took maybe two full days to realize that being home did not disappoint. I could feel my body relax in a way it hadn't for years. Moving home gave me the safety I was looking for. For my son, the closeness was life-changing in all the best ways.

"Papa, can you make soup?"

Basically, from the moment we moved, my son was obsessed with my dad's homemade chicken soup. It was an ordeal to prepare, but without fail, my father cooked it whenever his grandson asked. But that's not the only Papa-related craze my kid leaned into once we were home. Games of hide-and-seek, walks around the lake, making paper airplanes, and Papa's pancakes were a few of the favorites my kid loved about visiting his grandfather.

The best was after our weekly dinners, my son and I would climb into our car, and wave goodbye to my mom and dad from the driveway. As we drove out through the neighborhood and turned the corner, my dad would magically appear on the sidewalk, waving again. My son was astounded and laughed so hard, shouting, "Papa, how did you do that?!" I never told my kid that his grandfather had simply run through two backyards to catch us. I was happy to let him believe his Papa was magic.

Moving home gave us all more than we knew we needed — time together. Countless memories were made that wouldn't have happened had we stayed. Those taken-for-granted, everyday moments of homemade soup and silly waves are forever part of us. In the four years since my dad's sudden heart attack, this is what's helped us through our shock and grief. Our lives wouldn't be as full without them.

A retired student-loan borrower is fighting for public service debt relief. Now he's named in a lawsuit against Trump.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Donald Knierim is fighting to have his Public Service Loan Forgiveness processed.

Vanessa Valadez for BI

After working in public service for over a decade, Donald Knierim is still waiting for student-loan forgiveness.

Now he's participating in a lawsuit in hopes of getting relief for himself — and thousands of others.

Knierim first spoke to Business Insider earlier in October. In 2015, the 71-year-old started working for Metra, Chicago's commuter railway. His goal was to stay on the job for a decade in order to earn Public Service Loan Forgiveness, which forgives student debt for government and nonprofit workers after 10 years.

He thought he had succeeded in November 2025, when his Federal Student Aid dashboard read, "Congratulations! You've satisfied your obligation, and no additional payments are required for this loan."

Knierim received an initial notice that he achieved Public Service Loan Forgiveness.

Vanessa Valadez for BI

So he retired from Metra, with the belief that his student-loan payments were in the past. Months later, he received another notice: 15 payments that had previously counted toward his forgiveness progress were now rescinded, and his outstanding balance was reinstated.

On Friday, the American Federation of Teachers filed a lawsuit that includes Knierim as a declarant, alleging that the Department of Education illegally clawed back borrowers' PSLF payments and is stalling debt relief for thousands.

The stakes are high for Knierim; he cannot return to work because caring for his wife, who is dealing with serious health issues, is a full-time job.

"He lives on a fixed income, and diverting those funds to his student loans and away from mounting household expenses and savings puts his wife and him in a financially precarious situation," the lawsuit said.

Challenges with PSLF have mounted over the past few months, with borrowers reporting inaccurate payment counts and stalled applications to the buyback process, which allows a borrower to "buy back" time spent in deferment or forbearance if it would put them at the relief threshold.

Knierim is named in a lawsuit against the Education Department regarding stalled debt relief for thousands of PSLF borrowers.

Vanessa Valadez for BI

NFL keeps fining Caleb Williams for refusing to cover logo of "science" cleats worn by over 80 players

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The NFL says player safety is a top priority, but apparently, wearing cleats designed to help prevent injuries can cost you a pretty penny.

Chicago Bears quarterback Caleb Williams has been fined a whopping $35,823 over two games for wearing Caddix cleats, a company only a few years old with only four employees, which claims its technology can reduce the risk of non-contact injuries through its SmartStuds technology.

CEO Jack Rasmussen, after explaining that Williams is not, and has not been a Caddix sponsored athlete, nor has he ever been paid by the company, told OutKick, "I get why he was fined, 100%. I totally get it. We're not a league sponsor, and we can't afford to be or want to be a league sponsor."

Williams was initially fined $11,941 for displaying an "unauthorized logo" during Chicago's Week 6 victory over the Atlanta Falcons. The NFL then doubled down, issuing another $23,882 fine for the same violation against the New England Patriots in Week 7.

While he understands why the league fined Williams, Rasmussen made his company's support extremely clear saying, "Caleb Williams bought [our cleats], liked them, wore them, and now he's being penalized for liking them and wearing them, and we're taking a stance where we're going to support a player who's looking out for his best interest."

Rasmussen also said, "We're a small company. We've been selling cleats for less than 2 years. I have a total of four employees, so this is certainly an uphill battle for us, but it's a battle we're happy to be a part of."

The interesting aspect of this whole saga is the fact that Caleb Williams has worn these cleats before the Bears Week 6 match-up against the Atlanta Falcons, but he wasn't fined. I reached out to the NFL for an explanation but have yet to hear a response.

NIKE CEO MAY HAVE JUST ADMITTED THE COMPANY WENT TOO WOKE AS STOCK HITS 12-YEAR LOW

In case you weren't aware, the reason you only see Adidas, Nike, and Under Armour logos on NFL players' feet is because the NFL can charge those brands tens of millions of dollars per year to be "official apparel partners" of the league. While players can wear cleats from other manufacturers, such as Caddix or New Balance, which is worn by Josh Allen, they are expected to wear non-branded cleats like Allen does.

Notably, in the same vein, San Francisco 49ers running back Christian McCaffrey wears Bruce Bolt gloves but the lightning bolt logo is absent from the glove, as well as the company name.

Rasmussen also told me that McCaffrey's teammate Kyle Juszczyk is one of 80 players that has, or continues to wear Caddix cleats. "We have over 80 athletes wearing them. We haven't paid them a dollar," Rasmussen stated. "We actually have players actively leaving and turning down endorsement deals from other companies to wear Caddix for free. It's not about us finding the biggest guy, it's about allowing guys to wear this cleat because it's better for them."

Other NFL players that wear Caddix cleats include: Cooper Kupp, Bubba Baker, Marlon Humphrey, Jalen Ramsey, Derek Stingley Jr., and Dalton Kincaid.

As I told Rasmussen, "Those are some pretty notable names." Kupp is a former Super Bowl MVP, coming off another Super Bowl win. Ramsey was the highest paid cornerback in NFL history a few short seasons ago. It's an impressive list, especially when brands are knocking at these athletes doors to represent their brand and products.

CARDINALS BACKUP CORNERBACK FINED BY NFL FOR WRITING 'GLORY BE 2 GOD' ON HIS EYEBLACK

As far as Rasmussen is aware, not a single one of these 80+ players have been fined by the NFL like Caleb Williams has. If they were, I'm sure he'd be inclined to pay their fine just as he and his company have offered for Williams'.

Speaking directly to the lack of fines for other players, Rasmussen said, "People have been wearing Caddix cleats in NFL games for 3 years. Joe Flacco wore them all last year, had that record breaking game actually against the Bears, ironically enough, and then he was on Netflix wearing Caddix logos all over television, and no one said a word. I think it's just very precarious timing, because you have this player in Caleb Williams, who we all know is gonna be the face of the league here pretty soon."

I also reached out to the NFL for an explanation as to why Caleb Williams has been the only NFL player fined for wearing Caddix cleats. I have yet to receive a response.

Cincinnati Bengals quarterback Joe Flacco, who began wearing Caddix cleats in 2024 and subsequently became an investor in the company, has offered to cover Williams' future fines.

"If Caleb gets fined again for wearing Caddix, I'll pay the fine myself," Flacco told ESPN. "This isn't about making a statement or breaking the rules just to break them. It's about wearing gear that works for you and helps keep you safe on the field."

But more good news for Caddix from a marketing standpoint, Caleb Williams is not backing down and has essentially become a non-paid spokesperson for the brand.

"I'm huge into trying to stay on the field. I'm huge into trying to be healthy," Williams told reporters responding to news of his fines. "They're smart cleats. They're science cleats, and they've been working for me."

So what are these "science cleats" Williams and other NFL players swear by?

Caddix uses patented SmartStuds technology, featuring flexible studs designed to reduce rotational stress on players' knees and ankles during sudden cuts and movements. Williams believes the technology can help prolong his career, particularly after recently returning from a hamstring injury.

Rasmussen also mentioned, "Several very high profile guys who are currently signed to shoe companies buy cleats on our website."

With that information, of course I had to ask if any of those athletes are Nike athletes, which Rasmussen responded, "Yes, I certainly cannot tell you who it is though."

Williams has appealed his initial fine and reportedly intends to continue wearing the cleats without covering the logo, which will continue to be a headache for the NFL giving Caddix free marketing, but due to NFL rules, that could lead to the league barring Williams from playing.

In case you weren't aware, the NFL has uniform inspectors at every game to make sure players like Williams comply with specific branding standards, or at least, are made aware when they don't comply, making the fines not a surprise if they refuse to comply.

According to the NFL, "The uniform inspector’s job is to make sure that every player in violation of the rules is aware of it and has an opportunity to correct it. He’ll talk directly to players and, a half-hour before kickoff, he’ll meet with a team representative to report problems. That employee must bring any player spotted with a violation back to the inspector for clearance before kickoff." The Uniform Inspections section of the NFL website further states, "Players and teams have the opportunity to get into compliance at the next change of possession or other "appropriate stoppage" in play. After the game, the inspector roams the locker rooms, checking to make sure that no non-partner logos can be seen by reporters, fans or television cameras — and then writes a summary of each game for the league. For many violations, a player cannot go back into the game until the problem is corrected. Teams can be penalized 5 yards if — after receiving a warning — the player enters the game anyway. Failing to comply with league warnings can result in hefty fines."

Specifically, the NFL's collective bargaining agreement, Article 51, Section 2(a), specifically gives the league authority to regulate third-party branding on players' footwear. They are a business, big business, and they want to protect their business interests. That makes sense.

But does the NFL actually have the ability to bar Williams from entering the game if he refuses to comply on Monday Night Football against the defending Super Bowl champion Seahawks?

According to the NFL's Official Playing Rules, Rule 5, Section 4, players must comply with league uniform and equipment standards, including restrictions on unauthorized branding. The rule's penalty provisions allow officials to prevent players from entering a game if they refuse to correct certain uniform violations before kickoff. Players who fail to correct violations discovered during a game can also be prohibited from returning to the field.

That authority is reinforced by federal court documentation. In National Football League Management Council v. NFL Players Association (2016), the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit reproduced the NFL's uniform enforcement policy, which explicitly stated: "All uniform and On Field violations detected during the routine pregame check must be corrected prior to kickoff, or the offending player(s) will not be allowed to enter the game." The policy also required players to be removed from games until in-game violations were corrected.

In other words, while the NFL has not announced plans to keep Williams off the field over his Caddix cleats, its existing rules provide a potential enforcement mechanism that goes well beyond simply issuing another fine.

While speaking to Rasmussen on the potential of the NFL barring Williams from playing because he refuses to cover the Caddix branding, he jokingly said, "I think it's a great idea. I think they should definitely bench Caleb Williams."

He also said, in response to a question about whether or not he believes Williams will continue to defy the league's demands, "He seems like a guy who's just does what he wants to do, and if he does, good for him. If he doesn't, I get it too."

As for a potential partnership with Caleb Williams and Caddix in the future, Rasmussen said, "I met Caleb one time, and I've talked to him a handful of times, but he and I never talked about a deal ever." 

"Right now they're gonna focus on playing the season, you know? This isn't a conversation that needs to be had right now. We have to figure out if Caleb is gonna keep getting penalized and all that stuff, but I mean, we'll see. I would love to have a deal with Caleb Williams."

As for now, Caddix is a disrupter in the football cleats market. As Rasmussen said, "Obviously we want to disrupt, but I've had a number of conversations with most of the big brands in the past 12 months and some of them have become friends of mine. I got congratulations a couple of times last week from a certain company and a few of their employees, and it was great to hear."

"We're not gonna do things a conventional way. We're just not, and if it's gonna come down to safety or money, we're gonna put the first one every time, I promise."

Mark this down as another great American story of an entrepreneur using the tools and freedoms this great country has to offer to make a huge splash in the most profitable league in American sports, on the back of free advertising from one of the league's and American sports, brightest stars.
