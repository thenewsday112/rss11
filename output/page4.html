Ukraine is pushing fiber-optic drones to greater ranges. One maker questions whether that's worth the trade-offs.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Making Ukraine's fiber-optic drones fly further comes with trade-offs that might not be worth it, Ukrainian drone maker TechEx said.

Pavlo Bahmut/Ukrinform/Sipa USA via Reuters Connect

  • Ukraine's jam-resistant fiber-optic drones have been flying farther than ever.
  • But one maker questions whether more range is worth sacrificing payload capacity.
  • Longer cables add weight, potentially making some drones less useful in combat.

As companies continue to innovate, Ukraine's unjammable fiber-optic drones are flying farther and farther, but one company is questioning whether it's worth continuing to extend their range.

These drones, which can transmit commands and video through a physical cable, are not affected by enemy electronic warfare, specifically conventional radio-frequency jamming. Providing clearer video feeds than other FPV drones, they're useful for both surveillance and attack, and Ukrainian officials and companies have celebrated as their ranges have increased.

But TechEx, a major Ukrainian fiber-optic and interceptor-drone maker, told Business Insider that pushing those limits further may not be worth the trade-offs.

The company has built models with ranges of up to 35 and 40 kilometers. "We are currently considering whether it's still necessary to do the next versions of the fiber optic drones to fly, let's say, 45 or 50 kilometers," said Oleg, a TechEx executive who asked to be identified only by his first name for security reasons.

The reason behind the hesitation is that flying farther requires a heavier spool, as well as larger batteries to keep the added spool weight aloft, Oleg said. "This may affect the amount of the payloads carried by the drone."

"So we are still figuring out whether it's something that will be actually used on the front lines to do that long-range FPVs, or we will stick to, let's say, 40 or 45 kilometers."

Drone companies are constantly having to balance the weight of the cameras, explosives, weaponry attachments, communications gear, and other equipment. That balance is harder for fiber-optic drone makers to maintain because their drones are also carrying a spool of cable.

The fiber-optic cable and its spool add weight to fiber-optic drones that units and makers need to consider.

Jose Colon/Anadolu via Getty Images

Some Ukrainian drone makers have also said that shorter ranges are more effective anyway for fiber-optic drones because longer distances increase the risk of the cable breaking.

Describing TechEx's Stalker XO-10 and Stalker XO-15 drones, Ukraine's state-backed innovation platform Brave1 said the "fiber-optic drones have some limitations, including the need to carry a coil of cable that gradually unwinds during flight and provides communication with the operator."

"Since the coil itself has some weight, this can limit the payload of the UAV," it said, referring to uncrewed aerial vehicles.

Brave1 said that TechEx has so far been able to "mitigate this limitation" of the cable weight "through good engineering solutions" and assessed its work "allows these drones to achieve high payloads while maintaining the other advantages of fiber optics."

Whether that would continue to be true at longer ranges is a question mark right now.

Olha, a TechEx representative, told Business Insider that the company developed technology to make the spool lighter, making "tiny tweaks and changes" to how it's made and how the fiber is wound onto it. Ukraine's Ministry of Defense said last year that the Stalker series drones have a "record-low spool weight among Ukrainian fiber-optic drones."

Not all companies see the weight-to-range payoff the same way, and that could be good for Ukraine. Units use a host of drones from different companies for different missions, and soldiers say they like the flexibility to choose the equipment best suited to each mission. Some missions may call for longer-range drones carrying smaller payloads, while others require more firepower over shorter distances.

Fiber-optic drones come with big advantages, but also limitations.

Jose Colon/Anadolu via Getty Images

And attack missions aren't the only ones where explosive weight is a factor. Soldiers have described using fiber-optic drones for intelligence gathering with their onboard cameras. But there can still be a trade-off, with some higher-quality cameras weighing more.

Fiber-optic drones are just one part of Ukraine's growing arsenal, which includes long-range strike drones, mid-strike drones, and even heavy bombers. Many of these are able to carry much heavier payloads than small FPV drones. Each system comes with advantages and vulnerabilities, forcing manufacturers and soldiers to weigh which capabilities matter most for a given mission.

TechEx receives constant feedback and requests from the units that use its drones, which inform its decisions about what to build.

It has a technical support department, which Oleg says works with more than 100 units. It results in weekly changes to products ranging from small updates to major alterations.

Feedback, along with field testing by military pilots and operators, is critical to the work the company does, and has led it toward counterintuitive approaches, away from what it had initially thought would work best and would be what units want.

For instance, the prototype of the company's Striker interceptor drone was originally much bigger, with a 1.5-kilogram payload, but testing and feedback showed it needed to be smaller and more agile and didn't need such a large payload.

"We were able to completely redesign, and let's say eventually it looked nothing like the initial prototype," Olha said. Developing new weapons of war is a constant process of reevaluating, rethinking, and evolving.

A 'humbling' career move led to Catherine Zeta-Jones' big break

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Catherine Zeta-Jones currently stars in "Killing Jackie."

Getty; TriStar Pictures; USA Films; BI

Catherine Zeta-Jones' arrival in Hollywood in the mid-1990s was an ego check.

The Welsh actor, then in her 20s, had starred for years on the popular early-'90s British series "The Darling Buds of May," and was already a sensation in the UK. But when she touched down in California, she went from being recognized on the street to having casting agents ask her in auditions if she had any acting experience.

That wasn't all.

"I couldn't get cable TV," Zeta-Jones told Business Insider with a laugh. "I had been working since I was 9, but I couldn't buy anything in the States because I didn't have a credit card yet. I had to start from scratch."

Starting over turned out to be worth it for Zeta-Jones. After taking a role in the CBS miniseries "Titanic," she caught the attention of Steven Spielberg, who recommended her for "The Mask of Zorro." The role would make her a star Stateside and lead to an illustrious three-decade career in Hollywood.

Business Insider's "Role Play" series features interviews where acting legends revisit their most memorable roles. See more here.

Zeta-Jones is now known on both sides of the pond for memorable roles ranging from the pregnant wife of a Mexican drug lord in "Traffic" to her Oscar-winning turn as the iconic Velma Kelly in "Chicago" to Morticia Addams in Netflix's "Wednesday."

But despite her long list of credits, there's one thing she still hadn't done for a role until now: use her native Welsh accent.

Catherine Zeta-Jones in "Killing Jackie."

AMC+

Her latest project, the AMC+ series "Killing Jackie," marks Zeta-Jones' long-awaited return to her Welsh roots. After decades of having her brain work overtime doing English, American, and Scottish accents — and many more — the opportunity to speak in her native accent was grounding. "It was liberating and cathartic," Zeta-Jones said.

So, too, was the subject matter. Based on the 2018 novel "The Price You Pay" by Nick Harkaway (using the pseudonym Aidan Truhen), the revenge thriller stars Zeta-Jones as the titular Jackie Price, a Welsh high-end art dealer with a shady past in the drug trade who discovers a hit has been put out on her. With a deadly team of assassins known as The Seven Demons on her tail, Jackie goes to great lengths to stay alive — at one point even biting off a man's ear.

Navigating the series' ultra-violent scenes was a blast for Zeta-Jones, who relished the chance to go off the rails.

"I love this character," Zeta-Jones said. "I wanted the audience to come along on this ride with me and root for her. In all the craziness."

In the latest edition of Business Insider's Role Play series, Zeta-Jones reflects on her instant bond with Sean Connery, helping her husband Michael Douglas get a part in "Traffic," and the leading man she was most intimidated to work across from.

Zeta-Jones in 1997.

Frank Trapper/Corbis/Getty Images

On being discovered by Steven Spielberg and golfing with Sean Connery

Business Insider: Let's go back to your 20s. You're in London, where you can't walk down the street without paparazzi all over you thanks to "The Darling Buds of May." Then you go to LA, where nobody knows you. What was that like?

Catherine Zeta-Jones: I had six months on a visa, so I could go and give the States a shot for six months. I would have hated myself if I decided the American thing wasn't for me. I would have always thought, What if I had given it a shot?

It was good for me once I got over my ego being knocked a bit. I'd been in this business since I was 9 years old, professionally in theater and in television, and a few films in Britain at that point. But I'd go into casting agents, and they'd be asking me what I had done.

I took a job that was not a huge career leap for me — the CBS show, "Titanic," the TV series after the blockbuster movie came out. I kept reminding myself that I came to give America a shot.

Steven Spielberg, on a Sunday night, was watching this CBS "Titanic" series when he saw me. As the producer of "The Mask of Zorro," he suggested that I screen test. Literally, within a week of it coming on TV, I was shipped down to Mexico to screen test with Antonio Banderas. Five days later, they called my agent and said I got the job, and three days after that, I was down there starting my sword-fighting lessons. 

After that, you worked across from Sean Connery in "Entrapment," which cemented your stardom. Is it true you got that role because you told Connery you play golf?

[Laughs.] He used to say that tongue-in-cheek. You know, Sean Connery was a dear, dear force of nature. 

Would you guys play golf?

Yeah. 

Did you ever beat him?

No. And even if I could, I would never tell. I beat Michael Douglas. Any day of the week. But not Sean Connery.

You can't beat 007. 

No way. Even if he cheated, I would just turn my head. 

Zeta-Jones and Michael Douglas in 1999.

Ron Galella/Ron Galella Collection/Getty Images

On working with her husband on 'Traffic' and tap dancing with Christopher Walken

Speaking of your husband, is it true that Michael finally signed on to "Traffic" because he kept looking over your shoulder, looking at the drafts you were getting for the role you took in that movie?

There is a lot of truth in that. I was pregnant, and no one knew. And Steven Soderbergh asked me to do "Traffic." And I kept saying, "Oh my gosh, there's no way I can do this. Steven Soderbergh wants me in his movie, and I'm having a baby!" I was happy to be having a baby, but I so wanted to do this movie. So I called Steven and said, "I love the script, um, I think she would be even better if she was pregnant." And he said, "When are you due?" I went, "August," and he said, "We'll make it work." 

Michael never reads my scripts. I never read Michael's scripts. It's the one thing we kind of do on our own; we don't really talk shop at home. We just don't. But he knew how great that script was, and so Soderbergh called him and asked him if he had a chance to read my script. [Laughs.] And he said, "Kinda, but she's been telling me." So that's when he came on board. 

What I loved about that movie was not just the stellar performances, but I love that I can say I've been in a movie with Michael Douglas. Because if he wasn't my husband, I so would want to work with him. So even though we didn't have any scenes together, it was phenomenal. 

You starred in the rom-com "America's Sweethearts." The movie ends with Christopher Walken showing up to the junket to show footage of the movie he made with your character. It turns out to be all behind-the-scenes footage of the movie, which seems to be a musical, and we see you tap dancing with Walken. Is it true that subplot is in "America's Sweethearts" because you specifically wanted to tap dance with Christopher Walken?

Absolutely. I wanted to tap dance with Christopher Walken! I'm a big tap dancer. There are not many actors of my generation or Michael's generation, yes, [Michael Douglas' father] Kirk's generation, that can tap dance. So when I had the chance to do that, I was like, "Guys, we can't miss this chance." It was totally, selfishly, for myself. 

Zeta-Jones and Tom Hanks in "The Terminal."

DreamWorks

We touched on a few leading men you have worked with over your career. Looking back, is there one who made you stay on your game the most? Someone who you knew you had to bring your A-game to every time you walked on set?

I have to say Sean, but it wasn't that I had to be on my A-game. I just never wanted to stop acting with him, because we were such good buddies. He was so incredibly protective of me, like a daughter he never had, and he said that to me. And I loved his wife. I can't really say family, because he's not family, but I never wanted to act with anyone else after Sean Connery. I just didn't. It was fun. 

But I will say when I did "The Terminal" with Steven Spielberg at the helm and working so closely with Tom Hanks, I did go to work on the first day and go, "Mommy, I want to go home and cuddle." I got a little intimidated. Then — and I say this all the time — I learned the better they are, the nicer they are.

Now I've worked for so long, I've worked with some actors and gone, "Ugh, you just burst my bubble; I so wanted to work with you." Very rarely, but it has happened.

But working with all those guys on the "Oceans" movies: Matt [Damon], Brad [Pitt], George [Clooney]… I did a movie with George with the Coen brothers, and that was exceptional. So it's not so much bringing your A-game, because I want to do that with everyone I work across from.

Even on the movies where you realize, I don't think this is going to work, how do you get through it and feel positive and 100% committed? It's usually that the actors you're working with are so amazing.
