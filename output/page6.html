I'm running the NYC marathon along with four of my BI colleagues. These are our best tips for runners and spectators alike.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
BI's 2026 NYC marathon runners (left to right): Noah Sheidlower, Anthony DeMaio, Zak Jason, Kathleen Elkins, and Steve Russolillo

BI

  • This post originally appeared in the BI Today newsletter.
  • You can sign up for Business Insider's daily newsletter here.

Welcome to Marathon Sunday!

Yours truly will be among nearly 60,000 runners taking on the five boroughs today. I've spent months training for this one — with a little help from Muse, Meta's AI agent.

Several weeks ago, I wrote about how I had become addicted to Muse. Since then, Muse has become part of my marathon training, helping me map out long runs and shape an overall race plan. After the Staten Island half marathon last month, it even asked me, unprompted, "how'd the half go?"

The other day, I gave Muse my bib number and asked it to track my run. I'm hoping for real-time feedback and analysis, delivered straight through my AirPods, on how my race is unfolding and how I can stay on pace to achieve my goals.

But no AI agent can replicate the wisdom from real people.

At Business Insider, five of us are running the NYC marathon this year. We range from a first-time marathoner (Discourse editor Zak Jason) to a true veteran in Anthony DeMaio on the sales team, who will be running his 23rd NYC marathon (and roughly his 40th overall marathon; he says he's lost count!)

I asked them to share their best marathon advice for runners, spectators, or if you're simply trying to understand why someone would pay $330 to run 26.2 miles.

Here's what they had to say:

Noah Sheidlower, senior economy reporter: Live in the moment and acknowledge the spectators who came out to support. My race time last year was disappointing, but I had an absolute blast because I was high-fiving everyone and making people's days. Tap the "power up" marathon sign that little kids are holding, give a fist pump to strangers, and laugh when you see a funny sign that someone put a lot of effort into. The marathon shouldn't just be a celebration of your individual work but a community celebration honoring the greatest city on earth. Oh also, buy merch and don't feel guilty about it! I bit the bullet and got the blue jacket, and it looks so good.

Kathleen Elkins, investing correspondent: Soak it all in! Write your name on your shirt, absorb all of the hearty cheers, give the high-fives and the smiles! When you give New Yorkers energy, they'll give it right back.

Zak Jason, Discourse executive editor: As a first-time marathoner, I'm in no position to advise on anything — other than to trust the advice of people who have done hard things before you, because they want you to be awake to the gift of being alive. (Then again, someone also told me it's OK to pee yourself during the race.)

Anthony DeMaio, global head of sales: Can't wait to run my 23rd NYC Marathon. The marathon is a celebration of preparation. Every early morning and every tough summer long run builds the confidence to stand on the starting line. The days you don't want to run are often the ones that matter most, discipline is choosing what you want most over what you want right now. Embrace the hard moments; they prepare you for the miles when things get tough. And remember, running is a team sport. Let people support you, and support them in return. To all my fellow runners: May the wind be at your back, the sun on your shoulders, and the crowds carry you all the way to the finish.

And for me, my standard advice is that the first five miles can make or break a marathon. Go slower than your expected pace at the beginning. If you think you're going too slow at the onset, go even slower.

You'll thank yourself later!

A retired student-loan borrower is fighting for public service debt relief. Now he's named in a lawsuit against Trump.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
Donald Knierim is fighting to have his Public Service Loan Forgiveness processed.

Vanessa Valadez for BI

After working in public service for over a decade, Donald Knierim is still waiting for student-loan forgiveness.

Now he's participating in a lawsuit in hopes of getting relief for himself — and thousands of others.

Knierim first spoke to Business Insider earlier in October. In 2015, the 71-year-old started working for Metra, Chicago's commuter railway. His goal was to stay on the job for a decade in order to earn Public Service Loan Forgiveness, which forgives student debt for government and nonprofit workers after 10 years.

He thought he had succeeded in November 2025, when his Federal Student Aid dashboard read, "Congratulations! You've satisfied your obligation, and no additional payments are required for this loan."

Knierim received an initial notice that he achieved Public Service Loan Forgiveness.

Vanessa Valadez for BI

So he retired from Metra, with the belief that his student-loan payments were in the past. Months later, he received another notice: 15 payments that had previously counted toward his forgiveness progress were now rescinded, and his outstanding balance was reinstated.

On Friday, the American Federation of Teachers filed a lawsuit that includes Knierim as a declarant, alleging that the Department of Education illegally clawed back borrowers' PSLF payments and is stalling debt relief for thousands.

The stakes are high for Knierim; he cannot return to work because caring for his wife, who is dealing with serious health issues, is a full-time job.

"He lives on a fixed income, and diverting those funds to his student loans and away from mounting household expenses and savings puts his wife and him in a financially precarious situation," the lawsuit said.

Challenges with PSLF have mounted over the past few months, with borrowers reporting inaccurate payment counts and stalled applications to the buyback process, which allows a borrower to "buy back" time spent in deferment or forbearance if it would put them at the relief threshold.

Knierim is named in a lawsuit against the Education Department regarding stalled debt relief for thousands of PSLF borrowers.

Vanessa Valadez for BI

Ellen Keast, communications director for the Education Department's undersecretary, said in a statement that the PSLF buyback process requires "case-by-case reviews and extensive paperwork."

"FSA has already begun transitioning this work to loan servicers as part of a broader effort to streamline operations, reduce administrative burden, and modernize the process," Keast said. "Rather than acknowledge these realities, the union has yet again chosen litigation and election-year theatrics."

Knierim said he's grateful to be part of the lawsuit and to have "a strategic path forward."

"I have a flawless payment history," Knierim said. "I have never missed a payment, and I paid what they told me to pay."

The burden of stalled student-debt relief

Knierim has spent months seeking clarity on his account. He called and sent letters to Federal Student Aid and the ombudsman's office, and he even offered to pay a lump-sum amount if it would qualify him for PSLF.

He said no one ever responded to that offer. Worried that any further action on his account could jeopardize his loan forgiveness, he didn't make any payments during this time, so he was marked as delinquent and was set to default in November. The consequences of defaulting can be severe, including wage garnishment and the seizure of federal benefits like Social Security, which Knierim relies on.

Student-loan borrowers risk defaulting if they cannot afford payments while their relief is being processed.

Vanessa Valadez for BI

His forbearance request was approved days before he was set to default, and a "reconsideration request" on his PSLF status is in progress.

The AFT's lawsuit said that Knierim's dilemma is likely "widespread, affecting thousands, if not tens of thousands, of borrowers." One of the plaintiffs in the lawsuit, Elizabeth Schoepflin, had 14 payments toward PSLF rescinded, extending her repayment timeline by nearly a year and a half.

"Her servicer confirmed that there were no changes to her account that would explain the Department's clawback, and could not tell her why the Department took away the qualifying months," the lawsuit said.

Knierim said he's been diligent with his payments and that customer service representatives repeatedly assured him he was on track for loan forgiveness. He's hopeful he'll get the relief he's paid years toward and can continue his retirement with his student loans officially behind him.

"If I have to restore that payment, it's going to put my wife and me in a bad situation," Knierim said. "We're too tight in retirement to be shelling out money for stuff that we can't afford."

I run an 8-person startup that uses AI agents. We don't let our agents run on vibes — here's the playbook system we created

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Rachel Woods created playbooks full of AI agents to replace additional hires at her company.

Courtesy of Rachel Woods

  • Rachel Woods taught herself AI while studying business in college, later landing a role at Facebook.
  • She left to start her own business, which helps people integrate AI into their everyday workflows.
  • She said the company has saved itself at least 4 new hires by using AI agents and playbooks.

This as-told-to essay is based on a conversation with Rachel Woods, CEO and cofounder of AMP. The following has been edited for length and clarity.

My first official role in AI was at Facebook. I started as an intern and then joined full-time in 2018. I worked on AI research and development for the ads system that's powered by AI.

I don't have an engineering degree or background; I studied management information systems at The University of Texas at Austin. Part of why I think I got my role at Facebook was because I spent time building my own side projects and teaching myself how to train AI models and do data analysis.

I left Facebook in 2020 to start my own business in the wine industry, and after that company sold, I founded an AI company. I would need to double the number of people on my team if we didn't use AI playbooks.

If you're being thoughtful about setting up your AI operations, it's no different than building a team that you can trust.

That's something a lot of people are missing: how to actually make these systems things you can take your eyes off and not keep yourself up at night in the process.

Business Insider wants to talk to founders of AI-powered companies with fewer than 10 workers, and employees working alongside AI agents and bots, to understand what the "Tiny Teams" era really feels like — the wins, the fears, and the human skills that stand out. Share your story by filling out this quick form.

I've been an entrepreneur since the age of 15

My first business was a photography business, and I was too young to even drive to meet my clients. I would always catch rides with people, and my parents hated this.

I got into tech because in college, I was exposed to colleagues doing data analytics and working with coding and AI at summer internships. That led me to look online and try to take related classes, and I started to see that if you break a business problem into small enough pieces, you can get AI to handle parts of it: predicting, sorting, generating.

Later, when I was applying to Facebook, I used AI and data analysis to build a report of coffee shops in Austin that were less likely to be too busy for me to study at. I think that project helped me land my role.

After I left Facebook, I started a wine business called Vinebase in 2020; we built a tech platform that used AI to help winemakers sell their wine online and manage the digital side of their business.

Because this was before AI became more mainstream, we didn't market it as AI. We called it our "auto magic" system. I think that if we had said it was AI, it could've been a turnoff for our customers. Vinebase was acquired by Corksy in fall 2022.

I use my playbooks and AI agents to replace additional staff

My sister and I started AMP, originally called The AI Exchange, before ChatGPT was released in November 2022, because I was using AI in Vinebase and saw how the technology was progressing. ChatGPT came out about a month later and started really accelerating the AI timeline, so we went all in.

We have four full-time employees and four part-time contractors right now, but we'd need double if we weren't running as much as we are on AI. Given the company's current size, we'd need at least four to five additional people, including a sales and marketing manager, customer support specialist, executive assistant, and operations coordinator.

The way we structure these systems is based on something we call playbooking. Every department in the company has its own system. Each system is a collection of playbooks with AI agents that can run independently or together.

How we build our AI playbooks

To build an AI playbook, pick one piece of work you do over and over, and write down what starts it, what goes into the task, and what should come out at the end. Next, write the steps the way you'd train a new hire: each step, the decisions in it, and what "good" looks like.

Start running it with AI, and while you watch, compare the result to how you would've done it. Then, fix the playbook, not the output, until you trust it to run without you.

I always think through how I want a playbook to work and outline it by hand. Whenever you're making an AI playbook, you want to own the thinking and the logic. Otherwise, you can fall into the trap of including something that you don't really understand, and then you're not going to be able to trust that system anyway.

We have an AI agent running our sales playbook to review customer calls and ensure they meet our standards, rather than having a sales manager review them manually. Also, instead of sales team members needing to ask their manager questions, they just ask the playbook, which then pulls the answers from our guide for that team.

There are two types of AI costs we have in the business. The first is the subscriptions to Claude and ChatGPT for every person on the team. The other is because the majority of our operations are these AI playbooks that are running in the background without us being the ones starting or triggering it. In September, we spent $8,510 on Claude Code to run our playbooks in the background, which is billed by usage and is separate from the team's monthly Claude and ChatGPT subscription costs.

Own the playbook, rent the tech

We say "own the playbook, rent the tech," which means that if you are creating really good playbooks for your AI to follow, it actually doesn't matter which tool you're running it in.

Right now, we have a setup that's running with Claude Code in the background. But I could run all of these playbooks in ChatGPT, Grok, Gemini, or Microsoft Copilot.

We try to avoid a lot of the risks that people are concerned about with AI going rogue by creating these detailed outlines. We don't just want the agents to run on vibes. There still needs to be a person to decide and design how the work gets done; AI just speeds up everything else. That's the job security we have in this AI era.

Start small, and get everyone involved

A few of us on the team who are more advanced with AI own the bigger, team-wide playbooks that the business depends on, but everyone playbooks their own personal workflow, and then we use that as the testing ground to graduate things into becoming more part of official systems.

My advice would be to just start with something small and really easy, because once you get your first win and see how AI can do that repetitive work for you, it's kind of like a snowball.

Ready to read in color? These are the 4 best color e-readers.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When we write about e-readers, Kindle is usually the first name that comes to mind. But in the color e-reader market, Kindle has been late to the party. That delay opened the door for other brands to step in with stronger devices. We’ve rounded up the best color e-readers, and here are the ones that stood out.

Color e-reader price increases

This year has not been good for tech prices. Due to the global memory crisis, prices are increasing on tablets, phones, laptops, and even e-readers. Three out of the four color e-readers on this list increased in price this year. The only e-reader to avoid a price hike was the Kindle Scribe Colorsoft.

Temper your color expectations

An e-reader's color won't be quite as vibrant as a tablet's or physical book's. Credit: Samantha Mangino / Mangino

Having tested my way through a lot of e-readers — and now color e-readers — I’ve found a universal truth: While the colors are accurate, they often look a bit washed out. No matter which brand you use, that’s going to be the case. The muted tones don’t outweigh the convenience and portability of color e-readers, but it’s worth tempering your expectations.

Don’t expect the vivid, punchy colors of an iPad screen. An iPad has a max of 500 nits (a measure of brightness), while Kobo and Kindle e-readers max out at 100.

New to this guide:

  • As of October 2026, we added the new Kindle Colorsoft Signature Edition to this list. While the display and resolution haven't improved, its new aluminum casing makes it a sturdier e-reader.

  • In February 2026, we added the Kindle Scribe Colorsoft to this guide. After testing Amazon's latest e-reader, we were thoroughly impressed by both its speed and display.

What about ReMarkable tablets?

You might notice that ReMarkable e-ink tablets are not on this list. We've tested both the ReMarkable Paper Pro and ReMarkable Paper Move, and while they're dynamic digital notebooks, they're limited in their e-reader capabilities. Getting e-books on these devices was a bit of a struggle, and we missed the Libby integrations available on Kindles and Kobos. So while we think they're great e-ink tablets, they're inferior e-readers.

The Beats 360 are eye-catching and impressively customizable, but does the listening experience match up?

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you don't like the look of the Beats 360 headphones, you might as well click out of this review now.

That's not to say these headphones are only as good as their appearance — they do have their strengths — but if the many colorways Beats started teasing back in June via World Cup players wasn't enough of a hint, a big part of the allure of these Beats are how they look.

After spending a couple of weeks with these headphones, and switching out many an ear cushion and head band, I found myself more impressed with some aspects of these headphones, and surprisingly let down in others. While a solid first attempt at this concept, I have some notes — so let's get into it.

Beats 360 at a glance

The pink/blue combo demanded a cute background. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

The new flagship headphones from Beats run for $349.99 (the same intro price as the Beats Studio Pro) and come with the following features:

  • Battery life: 32 hours per charge

  • Active noise cancellation: Yes

  • Microphones: Nine, with eight dedicated to ANC

  • IP rating: IPX4

  • Connection: Bluetooth 5.3, one-touch pairing for Apple and Google Fast Pair for Android users

  • Colors: Black, cloud, sky, pink

The design of the Beats 360 can't be beat (so far)

The replaceable components meant allowed me to rock an all pink look. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

The Beats 360 aren't the first over-ear headphones to do swappable components: Dyson's OnTrac headphones launched with replaceable ear plates and cushions back in 2024. They did, however, also cost $499.99 and are less suited to using during exercise than the Beats.

The Beats cushion sets are the main draw of their design. The headphones themselves come in four different colors: black, cloud (cream), pink, and sky (light blue). Each headphone will come with its corresponding color in the Performance Knit cushion set, which closely resembles that found on the AirPods Max 2.

SEE ALSO: The 5 most comfortable headphones we've ever tested

You'll also have the option of purchasing an additional Performance Knit set in one of six different colors: black, cloud, pink, sky, navy, and volt (a neon green-yellow). Then there are two shades of the UltraPlush cushion set, which comes in black and cloud. The UltraPlush is the same faux leather material used on the Beats Studio Pro (and found on most over-ear headphones). That is to say, they're plenty comfortable for longer periods of wear and a nice alternative if you want to reserve the Performance Knit cushions for more high-activity use. All additional kits are priced at $49.99, but launch pricing has these marked down 80% for just $9.99.

The de-cushioned Beats surrounded by three cushioning options. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

It's plenty simple to swap out the cushions, which is why I was surprised by the case. These headphones encourage you to swap them out for different uses throughout the day, yet the magnetic closure case didn't consistently shut properly with an additional cushion set — sometimes it closed just fine, and sometimes I found myself needing to place the headphones and the components in the case just so if I didn't want it to hang open.

With the right positioning of the extra cushions, the case closed without issue. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable But sometimes, despite my best efforts, the case refused to close. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

Another oversight in the design I didn't love was the lack of a headphone jack and lack of USB-C listening functionality. It's not the end of the world, but it can limit listening options, especially while traveling or for anyone interested in lossless audio — a feature supported by the Beats Studio Pro.

Where the Beats 360 could definitely do better

The out-of-box sound on a pair of headphones could literally sound like thousands of angels singing in my ears, but if I'm paying $349.99, I would like the option to adjust my own sound signature. Maybe I want one less angel singing!

It's a concept Apple seems to understand with the recent iOS 27 update, which finally gave the AirPods Pro 3 a custom equalizer, but these Beats were left out of the software update.

Adjustable audio would've gone a long way for these customizable headphones. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

It's unfortunate, especially because the sound on these headphones not only didn't have the whole angel thing going on — it sounded kind of flat and bland, which feels decidedly un-Beats-like. The brand is known for its pumping bass, but here, the mids and vocals take center stage. Case in point: I was not bopping my head along to Lorde's "Supercut," which is a feat I frankly didn't know was possible. On a different point in the genre spectrum, Joni Mitchell's "Both Sides Now" sounded predictably beautiful because it's a Joni Mitchell song — the balance of sound, however, leaned too heavily on her vocals and shrank the guitar into the background. I found myself itching to adjust the equalizer settings while listening to the track.

The ANC isn't bad

Faring better than the sound is the ANC. Though it won't create the cocoon of silence some of Mashable's favorite noise-cancelling headphones are capable of, they'll definitely dim the sounds of a student-packed cafe playing loud punk music (trust me). That said, mid-range priced headphones are facing some serious competition from budget headphones these days, which are getting better and better at providing ANC that punches above its weight class.

The combo of the UltraPlush cushions and ANC made these headphones great for work. Credit: Bethany Allard / Mashable

There's also the presence of some hissing that's somewhat noticeable without music playing — it's not nearly as loud as some cheaper pairs of headphones, but if you're sensitive to ANC that hisses, it's worth noting.

Are the Beats 360 worth it?

At this point, the Beats 360 don't quite have the 360 appeal that they could with a few small changes. The component customization makes it easy to envision a world where these are the headphones people take to the gym instead of AirPods Max, while still being a great pair of headphones to use for work, school, commuting — true all-arounders.

But middling ANC, OK sound, and zero equalizer for $350 makes it hard to recommend these wholeheartedly to the majority of folks, especially when cheaper headphones can manage all of the above, even if they don't feature the same customization. I anticipate that gym rats will love them — I look forward to the next iteration when the rest of us can, too.
